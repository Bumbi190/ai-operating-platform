# Chapter 2 — System Position, Ownership and Boundaries

<a id="section-2-1"></a>
## 2.1 Chapter Status

This chapter defines the canonical system position, ownership model, responsibility boundaries and trust relationships of the Omnira Intelligence Fabric.

It establishes:

- where the Fabric belongs within Omnira,

- who may use it,

- who governs it,

- what responsibilities it owns,

- what responsibilities it must not absorb,

- how it relates to Atlas, Executive Intelligence, Managers, agents, workflows, projects and providers,

- and which architectural boundaries must remain intact as the platform evolves.

This chapter is normative for architecture direction after formal approval.

It does not prove that the described boundaries are currently implemented.

Runtime enforcement must be verified independently through code, configuration, policy, tests, deployment state and operational evidence.

No component receives additional execution authority merely because its relationship to the Fabric is described in this chapter.

<a id="section-2-2"></a>
## 2.2 The System Position of the Intelligence Fabric

The Omnira Intelligence Fabric is a shared platform-level intelligence execution service.

It exists between systems that express intent and resources that perform intelligence work.

Its canonical position is:

Human or Authorised System

↓

Intent, Objective or Work Request

↓

Atlas, Manager, Agent or Workflow

↓

Governed Capability Request

↓

Omnira Intelligence Fabric

↓

Models, Providers, Tools, Runtimes and External Services

↓

Execution Evidence, Outputs and Evaluation

↓

Calling System, Approval Boundary, Memory or Asset Library

The Fabric is not the origin of strategic intent.

It is not the final owner of business decisions.

It is not the permanent identity of an agent.

It is not a project-specific application.

It is not a provider marketplace acting independently of user interests.

It is the governed execution layer that converts approved capability requirements into controlled use of intelligence resources.

<a id="section-2-3"></a>
## 2.3 A Shared Omnira Platform Service

The Intelligence Fabric shall be implemented and governed as a shared Omnira platform service.

It shall not be duplicated independently inside:

- Familje-Stunden,

- The Prompt,

- GainPilot,

- Omnira’s own internal project,

- future customer projects,

- individual agents,

- isolated workflows,

- or temporary feature modules.

Projects may define their own:

- objectives,

- agents,

- workflows,

- brand rules,

- data classifications,

- budgets,

- provider restrictions,

- quality requirements,

- approval policies,

- assets,

- memories,

- and operational schedules.

They shall not need to build separate provider registries, credential stores, routing engines or execution evidence systems.

A common Fabric allows Omnira to improve intelligence access once and apply those improvements consistently across every authorised project.

This shared architecture must not be confused with shared data access.

The Fabric may be shared.

Project information, credentials, budgets, memories and assets must remain isolated according to their approved scope.

<a id="section-2-4"></a>
## 2.4 Ownership of the Fabric

The Intelligence Fabric is owned by the Omnira platform.

It is not owned exclusively by:

- Atlas,

- Executive Intelligence,

- a Manager,

- an individual project,

- an agent,

- a model provider,

- a connected account,

- or a developer tool.

Platform ownership means that the Fabric’s canonical contracts, policies, registries and execution boundaries are governed as part of Omnira’s foundational architecture.

Atlas may be the most prominent consumer of the Fabric, but Atlas shall not define provider access unilaterally.

A project may fund an execution, but it shall not rewrite the shared provider contract.

A provider may supply capabilities, but it shall not define Omnira’s authority model.

A connected development client may test a provider, but it shall not become the production owner of the integration.

The user remains the ultimate owner of the vision, connected resources and approved direction.

Omnira governs how those resources are used within the platform.

<a id="section-2-5"></a>
## 2.5 Governance Ownership and Runtime Ownership

The architecture shall distinguish governance ownership from runtime ownership.

Governance ownership defines who controls:

- canonical policies,

- provider eligibility,

- model eligibility,

- credential boundaries,

- data classifications,

- approval requirements,

- cost controls,

- conformance requirements,

- and lifecycle decisions.

Runtime ownership defines which component is currently responsible for:

- receiving a request,

- executing a plan,

- monitoring a job,

- handling callbacks,

- storing output,

- reporting status,

- and closing the execution.

The component that runs an operation does not automatically govern the rules under which it runs.

For example:

- a specialist video agent may coordinate production,

- OpenArt may perform a generation,

- a callback worker may receive the result,

- and an asset service may store the file.

None of those components independently owns the authority, budget or project policy governing the operation.

Governance remains explicit and separate.

<a id="section-2-6"></a>
## 2.6 The Fabric Is Not Atlas

Atlas and the Intelligence Fabric are separate architectural concepts.

Atlas is Omnira’s central coordinating intelligence.

Atlas may:

- understand user intent,

- interpret project context,

- reason about priorities,

- identify required capabilities,

- recommend action,

- coordinate Managers,

- request execution,

- review evidence,

- and communicate with the user.

The Fabric may:

- validate a capability request,

- enforce policy,

- resolve eligible resources,

- estimate cost,

- select providers and models,

- construct an execution plan,

- invoke adapters,

- monitor execution,

- handle fallback,

- record evidence,

- and return results.

Atlas determines what intelligence work is needed in support of the user’s approved direction.

The Fabric determines how that work may be executed within policy.

Atlas shall not hard-code:

- provider endpoints,

- provider credentials,

- model-specific billing logic,

- provider retry behaviour,

- provider-specific callback handling,

- or undocumented resource-selection rules.

The Fabric shall not independently decide:

- the user’s business strategy,

- project priority,

- long-term company direction,

- product positioning,

- or whether a consequential business action should occur without the required authority.

<a id="section-2-7"></a>
## 2.7 Atlas as a Fabric Consumer

Atlas shall access the Fabric through governed capability requests.

A request from Atlas shall include sufficient context to determine:

- who is requesting the work,

- which tenant and project are affected,

- what outcome is required,

- which data may be used,

- what authority has been granted,

- what budget applies,

- which quality threshold is required,

- and how the result should be returned.

Atlas shall not receive a special bypass around Fabric governance.

Atlas may have broader visibility than a project-specific agent, but broader visibility does not equal unrestricted execution authority.

Atlas’s access must remain constrained by:

- user ownership,

- tenant boundaries,

- project scope,

- capability permissions,

- data classification,

- budget,

- approval state,

- and operational policy.

A global coordinating role must not become an unbounded execution role.

<a id="section-2-8"></a>
## 2.8 Executive Intelligence and the Fabric

Executive Intelligence and the Intelligence Fabric serve different purposes.

Executive Intelligence is concerned with:

- strategic interpretation,

- decision quality,

- priorities,

- objectives,

- trade-offs,

- risks,

- expected value,

- project direction,

- and organisational alignment.

The Intelligence Fabric is concerned with:

- intelligence resource access,

- provider and model eligibility,

- execution planning,

- routing,

- cost,

- quality enforcement,

- data placement,

- reliability,

- evidence,

- and technical completion.

Executive Intelligence may determine that Familje-Stunden should prioritise a Nova and Pling pilot because it may improve discovery and subscription growth.

The Fabric may determine which approved image, voice and video resources can produce the pilot within the budget and rights requirements.

Executive Intelligence decides why the work matters.

The Fabric determines how the required intelligence resources can be used compliantly.

Neither layer shall silently absorb the other’s responsibility.

<a id="section-2-9"></a>
## 2.9 The Fabric Is Not Executive Intelligence

The Fabric shall not be treated as an executive decision-maker.

Provider performance data may show that one model is cheaper or more reliable.

That does not mean the Fabric may decide that a project should change its business model.

Audience analytics may show that one content format performs better.

That does not mean the Fabric may independently redirect the project strategy.

The Fabric may provide:

- execution evidence,

- quality evidence,

- cost evidence,

- provider evidence,

- and operational recommendations.

Executive Intelligence and authorised humans determine how that evidence affects strategic direction.

The Fabric supports decisions.

It does not own them.

<a id="section-2-10"></a>
## 2.10 Managers and the Fabric

Managers coordinate bounded areas of work within Omnira.

A Manager may:

- translate an approved objective into tasks,

- sequence work,

- assign specialist agents,

- track progress,

- identify dependencies,

- request intelligence capabilities,

- and escalate exceptions.

A Manager shall use the Fabric when intelligence execution is required.

The Manager shall not create private provider integrations outside Fabric governance.

The Manager shall not retain provider credentials.

The Manager shall not choose an ineligible provider merely to complete a task faster.

The Manager may specify legitimate work requirements such as:

- output deadline,

- quality target,

- required modality,

- project budget,

- approved production envelope,

- and task dependencies.

The Fabric shall resolve the eligible implementation.

Managers coordinate work.

The Fabric coordinates intelligence resources.

<a id="section-2-11"></a>
## 2.11 Specialist Agents and the Fabric

Specialist agents perform defined roles within Omnira.

Examples may include:

- a research agent,

- a software agent,

- a design agent,

- a video-production agent,

- a voice agent,

- a quality-review agent,

- a publication-preparation agent,

- or an application-specific agent such as Arnold.

A specialist agent shall not be permanently coupled to one model.

Its identity, instructions, memory access and authority shall remain Omnira-governed.

The agent may request capabilities such as:

- advanced reasoning,

- source verification,

- image generation,

- code execution,

- transcription,

- narration,

- or video animation.

The Fabric may fulfil different requests using different models or providers.

An agent’s capability access must be explicit.

The fact that a model can perform a task does not mean the agent is authorised to request it.

The fact that an agent is authorised to generate a draft does not mean it is authorised to publish the result.

<a id="section-2-12"></a>
## 2.12 External Provider Agents

Some providers may expose their own agents, assistants or autonomous workflows.

These external agents shall be treated as provider resources, not as sovereign Omnira identities.

An external provider agent may perform work within a bounded execution.

It shall not automatically receive:

- persistent Omnira identity,

- direct access to long-term memory,

- authority over other agents,

- cross-project visibility,

- unrestricted tools,

- independent spending authority,

- or permanent workflow control.

External agents must be represented through explicit capability and trust declarations.

The Fabric must record:

- which external agent was used,

- which provider operated it,

- what data it received,

- what tools it could access,

- what authority envelope applied,

- what result it produced,

- and what evidence was returned.

Provider terminology must not override Omnira’s governance model.

A provider may call a feature an “agent.”

Omnira shall determine what authority that feature actually possesses.

<a id="section-2-13"></a>
## 2.13 Workflows and the Fabric

A workflow defines a repeatable sequence of governed steps.

A workflow may include:

- human actions,

- agent tasks,

- provider calls,

- approvals,

- data transformations,

- evaluations,

- publication preparation,

- and external side effects.

Workflows shall access intelligence through the Fabric.

A workflow definition may specify capability requirements, but it should avoid embedding permanent provider-specific logic unless a pinned dependency is explicitly approved.

For example, a Nova and Pling production workflow may request:

- story segmentation,

- scene planning,

- image-to-video animation,

- Swedish narration,

- sound design,

- quality review,

- format conversion,

- and social-media extraction.

The workflow may require character-consistent video generation.

It should not assume that one specific provider will permanently supply that capability.

The Fabric shall select eligible resources according to current policy and evidence.

A workflow may intentionally pin a provider when a unique feature is required.

That dependency must be explicit and observable.

<a id="section-2-14"></a>
## 2.14 Applications and Project Services

Applications such as Familje-Stunden, The Prompt and GainPilot shall consume intelligence through project-scoped services or approved Fabric interfaces.

Application code should not directly contain:

- unrestricted provider keys,

- global provider-selection logic,

- hidden cost rules,

- provider-specific memory,

- or cross-project intelligence state.

An application may expose product-specific interfaces.

For example:

- Familje-Stunden may expose a monthly story-production interface.

- The Prompt may expose an AI-news production interface.

- GainPilot may expose exercise-visual and coaching interfaces.

These interfaces may be specialised.

Their intelligence execution must still pass through shared Fabric contracts.

Application specialisation belongs above the Fabric.

Provider specialisation belongs below the Fabric.

The Fabric is the boundary between them.

<a id="section-2-15"></a>
## 2.15 Human Users and Operators

Human users remain the source of legitimate vision, ownership and consequential authority.

The Fabric shall support different human roles, including:

- owner,

- administrator,

- project owner,

- operator,

- reviewer,

- approver,

- developer,

- auditor,

- and future customer roles.

A human user’s authority shall be explicit.

The system must not assume that every authenticated user may:

- connect providers,

- view all credentials,

- approve spending,

- access all projects,

- use all capabilities,

- publish content,

- or change routing policies.

Human authority may vary by:

- tenant,

- project,

- capability,

- provider,

- data class,

- budget,

- environment,

- and action type.

The Fabric must preserve these distinctions through the entire execution lifecycle.

<a id="section-2-16"></a>
## 2.16 The Fabric Control Plane

The Intelligence Fabric shall include a control plane.

The control plane governs the configuration and policy under which intelligence resources may be used.

Its responsibilities may include:

- Capability Registry,

- Model Registry,

- Provider Registry,

- Agent and Tool Registry integration,

- adapter registration,

- credential references,

- provider account configuration,

- routing policies,

- quality requirements,

- budget policies,

- data-placement policies,

- provider eligibility,

- model lifecycle,

- health state,

- approval rules,

- and conformance status.

Control-plane actions are generally administrative or governance-sensitive.

Examples include:

- connecting a new provider,

- adding an OAuth account,

- activating a model,

- changing a project budget,

- approving a provider for confidential data,

- deprecating an adapter,

- or changing fallback policy.

Control-plane actions must not be confused with ordinary execution requests.

<a id="section-2-17"></a>
## 2.17 The Fabric Data Plane

The Intelligence Fabric shall include a data plane.

The data plane performs governed intelligence execution.

Its responsibilities may include:

- receiving capability requests,

- resolving scope,

- validating authority,

- evaluating policy,

- planning execution,

- reserving budget,

- selecting resources,

- invoking adapters,

- managing asynchronous jobs,

- processing callbacks,

- handling retries,

- applying fallback,

- validating outputs,

- recording evidence,

- returning results,

- and releasing or finalising cost reservations.

The data plane shall operate only within control-plane policy.

A data-plane component must not redefine provider eligibility or grant itself additional authority during execution.

<a id="section-2-18"></a>
## 2.18 The Evidence Plane

The architecture shall recognise execution evidence as a distinct concern.

The evidence plane may include:

- execution records,

- traces,

- provider receipts,

- cost records,

- input references,

- output references,

- quality evaluations,

- human reviews,

- approval events,

- policy decisions,

- errors,

- retries,

- fallback events,

- and derived learning candidates.

Evidence supports:

- audit,

- debugging,

- cost attribution,

- provider evaluation,

- memory,

- decision support,

- incident response,

- and architecture improvement.

Evidence storage must preserve project and tenant scope.

Observability must not create a secondary path for data leakage.

Sensitive prompts or assets must not be copied into unrestricted logs merely for convenience.

<a id="section-2-19"></a>
## 2.19 Separation Between Control, Execution and Evidence

The control plane, data plane and evidence plane shall remain logically distinct.

They may share infrastructure where appropriate, but their responsibilities must not collapse into one unstructured service.

The control plane determines what may be used.

The data plane performs approved execution.

The evidence plane records what occurred.

This separation prevents several failure modes:

- execution workers silently changing policy,

- logs being treated as authoritative configuration,

- providers being activated through accidental use,

- temporary runtime state becoming permanent governance,

- and evidence records being mistaken for approval.

Each plane must have clear ownership, interfaces and access controls.

<a id="section-2-20"></a>
## 2.20 The Intelligence Fabric Boundary

The Fabric boundary begins when an authorised caller submits a governed capability request.

The Fabric boundary includes the controls required to:

- understand the request,

- determine eligibility,

- select resources,

- execute work,

- monitor progress,

- validate results,

- preserve evidence,

- and return an outcome.

The Fabric boundary ends when:

- an output is returned to the caller,

- an asset is handed to an approved asset service,

- an evaluation is recorded,

- a learning candidate is submitted to memory governance,

- or an external action is transferred to another governed subsystem.

The Fabric does not automatically own every downstream use of an output.

For example:

- the Fabric may create a publication-ready video,

- but a publication service owns the actual platform submission;

- the Fabric may generate code,

- but the repository workflow owns commit and pull-request governance;

- the Fabric may produce a recommendation,

- but Executive Intelligence owns strategic interpretation;

- the Fabric may produce a memory candidate,

- but Atlas Memory owns memory admission and lifecycle.

<a id="section-2-21"></a>
## 2.21 Boundary With Atlas Memory

The Intelligence Fabric and Atlas Memory shall remain separate systems.

The Fabric produces:

- execution evidence,

- generated outputs,

- quality evaluations,

- provider performance evidence,

- cost evidence,

- and learning candidates.

Atlas Memory governs:

- what information should persist,

- memory type,

- project scope,

- confidence,

- retention,

- contradiction,

- supersession,

- retrieval,

- and deletion.

The Fabric shall not automatically convert every prompt, response or provider result into permanent memory.

A failed generation may be useful execution evidence without being meaningful long-term memory.

A provider preference observed once may be a hypothesis, not a stable learning.

A repeated and evaluated pattern may become a governed memory after the appropriate process.

The Fabric supplies evidence.

Memory governs persistence.

<a id="section-2-22"></a>
## 2.22 Boundary With the Intelligence Graph

The Intelligence Graph may represent relationships generated by Fabric activity.

Relevant entities may include:

- capability requests,

- agents,

- workflows,

- providers,

- models,

- tools,

- executions,

- approvals,

- outputs,

- assets,

- decisions,

- evaluations,

- incidents,

- and learning.

The Graph may help Omnira understand:

- which provider produced an asset,

- which execution supported a decision,

- which agent requested a capability,

- which approval permitted a cost,

- or which model failure triggered fallback.

The Graph does not perform the execution merely because it represents it.

The Graph does not grant authority merely because an entity is connected.

The Fabric owns governed intelligence execution.

The Intelligence Graph owns canonical relationship representation within its approved scope.

<a id="section-2-23"></a>
## 2.23 Boundary With the Asset Library

Generated images, videos, audio files, documents and other media may be transferred to an Omnira asset-management service or project asset library.

The Fabric shall create or supply the required provenance.

The asset system shall govern:

- durable storage,

- file organisation,

- versions,

- canonical asset status,

- derivative relationships,

- archive state,

- retention,

- distribution references,

- and user-facing retrieval.

The Fabric may retain execution references.

It should not become an unstructured permanent file repository.

For Nova and Pling, the distinction is important:

- character references belong in the governed project asset structure,

- generation attempts belong in execution evidence,

- approved final scenes belong in the asset library,

- and published derivatives belong in distribution history.

These objects are related but not identical.

<a id="section-2-24"></a>
## 2.24 Boundary With Publication Systems

The Intelligence Fabric may prepare publication material.

It may generate:

- final media,

- captions,

- descriptions,

- metadata,

- thumbnails,

- platform variants,

- subtitles,

- and publication recommendations.

Publication itself shall remain a separate governed capability or service.

Publishing may create consequential external side effects.

The Fabric must not infer publication authority from generation authority.

An approved production envelope may permit:

- generation,

- review preparation,

- and export.

It may still prohibit public publication.

A separate approval or delegated publication mandate may be required.

The system must represent the difference between:

- generated,

- approved,

- ready for publication,

- scheduled,

- submitted,

- published,

- and verified live.

<a id="section-2-25"></a>
## 2.25 Boundary With Repository and Software Delivery Systems

The Intelligence Fabric may provide coding, analysis and testing capabilities.

It may generate:

- source code,

- tests,

- migrations,

- documentation,

- architecture reviews,

- and implementation plans.

Software-delivery governance remains separate.

The Fabric shall not assume authority to:

- modify a protected branch,

- commit changes,

- push to a remote repository,

- create a pull request,

- merge a pull request,

- deploy production code,

- or alter infrastructure.

Those actions belong to repository, CI/CD, approval and deployment systems.

A coding model may propose a change.

A governed development workflow determines whether and how that change enters the repository.

<a id="section-2-26"></a>
## 2.26 Boundary With Authentication and Identity

The Fabric shall rely on authoritative identity and authentication systems.

It shall not create independent user identities merely to simplify provider access.

The Fabric must be able to resolve:

- user,

- tenant,

- project,

- service identity,

- agent identity,

- workflow identity,

- provider account,

- and delegated authority.

Provider identities must be linked to Omnira identities without becoming equivalent to them.

An OAuth account connected to OpenArt is a provider credential relationship.

It is not the identity of Atlas.

An ElevenLabs workspace is a provider account.

It is not the owner of the Omnira project.

Identity translation must remain explicit.

<a id="section-2-27"></a>
## 2.27 Boundary With Credential and Secret Management

The Fabric may reference credentials.

It shall not expose credential values unnecessarily.

Secrets should be controlled through an approved vault or credential service.

The Fabric may receive:

- a credential reference,

- provider account identifier,

- permitted scope,

- token status,

- expiry state,

- and execution permission.

It should not require agents, prompts or ordinary application code to read raw secrets.

Credentials shall remain isolated by:

- tenant,

- owner,

- provider,

- account,

- project where required,

- environment,

- capability,

- and permitted action.

The Fabric must not copy secrets into:

- prompts,

- logs,

- memory,

- asset metadata,

- user-visible error messages,

- or provider-selection evidence.

<a id="section-2-28"></a>
## 2.28 Boundary With Billing and Financial Systems

The Fabric shall estimate, reserve, attribute and report intelligence execution cost.

It may record:

- provider price,

- estimated usage,

- actual usage,

- credits consumed,

- currency,

- budget reservation,

- project attribution,

- and cost variance.

The Fabric is not necessarily the authoritative accounting ledger for the entire organisation.

Formal financial systems may remain responsible for:

- invoices,

- tax,

- revenue recognition,

- payment processing,

- refunds,

- supplier reconciliation,

- and statutory accounting.

The Fabric must provide reliable cost evidence to those systems.

It must not fabricate precision when provider billing data is delayed or incomplete.

Estimated cost and confirmed cost must remain distinguishable.

<a id="section-2-29"></a>
## 2.29 Boundary With External Providers

External providers exist outside Omnira’s trust boundary.

Even when a provider is approved, Omnira must not assume that the provider shares Omnira’s internal governance.

A provider may control:

- infrastructure,

- model execution,

- rate limits,

- retention behaviour,

- model updates,

- moderation,

- regional processing,

- service availability,

- and billing records.

Omnira controls:

- whether the provider is eligible,

- which account is used,

- what data may be sent,

- what capability may be requested,

- what budget applies,

- how the result is validated,

- and how evidence is preserved.

Provider claims should be recorded and verified where possible.

A provider’s marketing description is not sufficient proof of:

- quality,

- privacy,

- commercial rights,

- deterministic behaviour,

- or availability.

External providers shall remain untrusted execution dependencies operating within explicit controls.

<a id="section-2-30"></a>
## 2.30 Provider Accounts and Workspaces

The architecture shall distinguish a provider from a provider account.

One provider may have multiple connected accounts or workspaces.

Examples may include:

- a user-owned personal account,

- an Omnira production account,

- a Familje-Stunden project workspace,

- a development sandbox,

- or a future customer-owned account.

Each account may have different:

- billing,

- credits,

- permissions,

- assets,

- data policies,

- regions,

- rate limits,

- and commercial terms.

The Provider Registry shall represent the provider.

Provider Account objects shall represent connected operational relationships.

The Fabric shall select both:

- an eligible provider,

- and an eligible provider account.

It must not assume that all accounts at the same provider are interchangeable.

<a id="section-2-31"></a>
## 2.31 Development Clients and the Fabric

Development tools such as Claude Code, Codex, IDE agents or manually connected MCP clients may help build, test and evaluate Fabric integrations.

They shall not become hidden production dependencies.

A Claude connection to an OpenArt MCP server may be useful for:

- testing video generation,

- evaluating character consistency,

- learning the provider’s capabilities,

- measuring credit use,

- and developing production prompts.

The resulting evidence may inform Fabric design.

The connection itself is not the production Fabric integration.

Production requires Omnira-controlled or Omnira-governed:

- authentication,

- scope,

- policy,

- cost tracking,

- execution records,

- asset handling,

- approval,

- and failure behaviour.

Development convenience must not redefine production architecture.

<a id="section-2-32"></a>
## 2.32 Manual Integration Boundary

Some providers may not expose a suitable API or MCP interface.

Omnira may still support them through a manually assisted workflow.

A manually assisted integration may include:

- preparing inputs,

- generating prompts,

- organising references,

- estimating cost,

- instructing a human operator,

- receiving uploaded output,

- and recording provenance.

The system shall label the integration truthfully.

Valid states may include:

- native automated,

- automated through MCP,

- automated through API,

- partially automated,

- human-assisted,

- manual external,

- experimental,

- unavailable,

- or deprecated.

A manual provider must not appear as if it were automatically controlled.

Truthful maturity status is part of the system boundary.

<a id="section-2-33"></a>
## 2.33 Tenant Boundary

Every Fabric operation shall execute within an explicit tenant boundary.

A tenant boundary governs:

- identity,

- credentials,

- provider accounts,

- budgets,

- policies,

- assets,

- memory,

- evidence,

- and administrative authority.

The Fabric shall not rely on naming conventions alone to isolate tenants.

Tenant isolation must be enforced through architecture and verified controls.

A tenant identifier must propagate through:

- capability request,

- policy evaluation,

- resource selection,

- execution,

- evidence,

- output storage,

- cost attribution,

- and memory submission.

Missing tenant scope shall not silently default to unrestricted access.

The safe default is denial or explicit failure.

<a id="section-2-34"></a>
## 2.34 Project Boundary

Within a tenant, every project-scoped execution shall identify its project.

Project scope determines:

- which assets may be used,

- which memories may be retrieved,

- which agents may participate,

- which provider accounts are eligible,

- which budgets apply,

- which policies apply,

- where outputs are stored,

- and who may review the result.

The active project must be resolved from an authoritative source.

It must not be inferred from:

- visual branding,

- a page title,

- a prompt string,

- a folder name alone,

- or a hard-coded application constant.

A request for Familje-Stunden must not inherit The Prompt’s assets, activities, schedules or pipeline state.

Project isolation is a correctness requirement, not merely a user-interface preference.

<a id="section-2-35"></a>
## 2.35 Global Platform Scope

Some Fabric operations may legitimately use a global platform scope.

Examples may include:

- provider health checks,

- model benchmarking,

- adapter conformance tests,

- global cost analysis,

- shared infrastructure monitoring,

- or platform-level routing evaluation.

Global scope must be explicit.

It must not be used as a convenient fallback when project scope is missing.

A platform-global execution may observe shared provider behaviour.

It shall not automatically receive access to all project content.

Atlas may hold an approved global view while still requesting project-specific execution through explicit project scope.

<a id="section-2-36"></a>
## 2.36 Cross-Project Workflows

Cross-project workflows may be legitimate.

For example:

- Omnira may create a portfolio-level performance report,

- The Prompt may report on a public Omnira launch,

- or a shared brand asset may be authorised for several projects.

Cross-project access must never be accidental.

A cross-project request shall declare:

- participating projects,

- permitted data types,

- purpose,

- authority,

- output scope,

- memory scope,

- and retention.

The Fabric shall not combine project context merely because the same user owns both projects.

Ownership alone does not remove project boundaries.

Cross-project access requires an explicit governed relationship.

<a id="section-2-37"></a>
## 2.37 Environment Boundaries

The Fabric shall distinguish environments such as:

- local development,

- test,

- preview,

- staging,

- production,

- recovery,

- and simulation.

Credentials, provider accounts, data and budgets may differ between environments.

A development execution shall not silently use production credentials.

A test workflow shall not publish to a live account.

A provider callback from a sandbox environment shall not update production records.

Simulation output shall not be represented as provider execution.

Environment identity must propagate through the full execution lifecycle.

<a id="section-2-38"></a>
## 2.38 Authority Boundary

Capability access and execution authority are separate.

A component may know that a capability exists without being authorised to use it.

A user may be authorised to generate an image but not to clone a voice.

An agent may be authorised to create a draft but not to spend beyond a defined amount.

A Manager may coordinate tasks but not connect a new provider.

A project may use public-data research but not external processing of confidential information.

The Fabric shall evaluate authority before execution.

Authority must include:

- subject,

- capability,

- action,

- scope,

- budget,

- data class,

- provider constraints,

- validity period,

- and approval state where applicable.

Missing authority shall fail closed.

<a id="section-2-39"></a>
## 2.39 Capability Boundary

The Fabric shall expose capabilities rather than unlimited provider access.

A caller may receive permission for:

- text generation,

- code review,

- image editing,

- or speech synthesis.

That permission must not imply unrestricted access to every tool offered by the same provider.

A provider integration may expose:

- generation,

- deletion,

- account management,

- asset listing,

- publication,

- cloning,

- billing,

- and administrative operations.

Each tool or operation must be separately classified.

Connecting a provider does not authorise every provider capability.

<a id="section-2-40"></a>
## 2.40 Budget Boundary

Every paid execution shall belong to a budget scope.

A budget scope may be:

- per request,

- per production,

- per agent,

- per workflow,

- per project,

- per tenant,

- per provider,

- or platform-wide.

Budget availability does not create authority.

Authority does not guarantee budget availability.

Both must be satisfied.

The Fabric shall not transfer cost between projects merely to complete a request.

It shall not silently use a user’s personal credits when an Omnira-managed budget is exhausted unless such fallback is explicitly approved.

It shall not convert a cost estimate into confirmed cost without evidence.

<a id="section-2-41"></a>
## 2.41 Data Boundary

Input data shall remain governed throughout execution.

The Fabric must track:

- source scope,

- classification,

- permitted processors,

- transformation,

- derivative classification,

- output scope,

- and retention.

A public output generated from confidential source material may still require confidential provenance and restricted execution evidence.

A summarised output does not automatically remove the sensitivity of its source.

A provider must not receive more context than required.

Data minimisation is part of the Fabric boundary.

<a id="section-2-42"></a>
## 2.42 Rights and Consent Boundary

Technical capability does not create legal or ethical permission.

The Fabric may be technically able to:

- clone a voice,

- imitate a visual style,

- generate a human likeness,

- transform copyrighted source material,

- or produce commercial media.

Execution requires appropriate rights, consent and policy.

The Fabric shall distinguish:

- provider terms,

- user ownership,

- project licence,

- individual consent,

- commercial-use eligibility,

- source restrictions,

- and publication rights.

A provider’s commercial-use allowance does not prove that the user owns every input asset.

Rights must be evaluated across the full production chain.

<a id="section-2-43"></a>
## 2.43 Quality Boundary

The Fabric is responsible for enforcing the quality contract of the execution.

It is not automatically responsible for determining the final subjective value of every result.

Quality may be assessed through:

- automated tests,

- model-based evaluation,

- deterministic validation,

- human review,

- project-specific rules,

- and performance evidence.

A technically completed generation may fail the quality boundary.

For example:

- a video may render successfully but distort Nova’s face,

- a narration may be intelligible but use the wrong emotional tone,

- code may compile but violate architecture,

- research may be fluent but unsupported.

Completion and acceptance must remain separate states.

<a id="section-2-44"></a>
## 2.44 Publication Boundary

The Fabric may produce publication-ready assets.

Publication requires its own authority.

The architecture shall not treat the following states as equivalent:

- generated,

- validated,

- reviewed,

- approved,

- publication-ready,

- scheduled,

- submitted,

- published,

- and verified live.

An execution receipt must report the actual state.

The phrase “completed” must not hide whether the intended external outcome occurred.

<a id="section-2-45"></a>
## 2.45 Learning Boundary

The Fabric may derive operational learning from evidence.

Learning must not cross scope without authority.

A routing improvement derived from general provider latency may be platform-wide.

A lesson derived from Nova and Pling character behaviour may belong to Familje-Stunden.

A customer-specific style preference must not become a global default.

A confidential project pattern must not train unrelated project decisions.

Learning scope shall be explicit and governed.

<a id="section-2-46"></a>
## 2.46 Provider Independence Boundary

Provider independence does not require removing all provider-specific behaviour.

Some providers offer unique strengths.

The Fabric may support provider-specific extensions through explicit capability declarations.

The boundary requires that provider-specific logic remains:

- isolated in adapters or approved extension points,

- visible in dependency records,

- testable,

- replaceable where possible,

- and prevented from leaking into every caller.

Provider-specific value is permitted.

Provider-specific architectural ownership is not.

<a id="section-2-47"></a>
## 2.47 Internal Trust Zones

The Fabric architecture should recognise distinct internal trust zones.

Potential zones include:

1. User and operator interfaces.

2. Atlas and orchestration systems.

3. Fabric control plane.

4. Fabric execution workers.

5. credential and secret services.

6. evidence and audit services.

7. project asset stores.

8. local compute nodes.

9. external provider boundaries.

10. publication and side-effect systems.

Movement between zones must be explicit.

A component inside Omnira is not automatically trusted for every action.

Least privilege applies internally as well as externally.

<a id="section-2-48"></a>
## 2.48 Fail-Closed Boundary Behaviour

When required scope, identity, authority or policy cannot be resolved, the Fabric shall fail closed.

Examples include:

- missing tenant,

- unknown project,

- unresolved credential owner,

- absent data classification,

- expired approval,

- uncertain provider terms,

- missing budget,

- or incompatible output rights.

The system shall not silently substitute:

- global scope,

- a default project,

- a personal provider account,

- a cheaper unapproved model,

- or a broader data policy.

Operational convenience must not weaken boundaries.

<a id="section-2-49"></a>
## 2.49 Boundary Enforcement Must Be Architectural

Boundaries shall not rely only on:

- prompt instructions,

- developer discipline,

- naming conventions,

- user-interface filtering,

- or assumptions that agents will behave correctly.

Prompt-level instructions may support behaviour.

They do not replace architectural enforcement.

Project isolation, credential isolation, authority, budget and data placement require enforceable controls.

The system should assume that:

- code may contain defects,

- agents may misunderstand,

- providers may change,

- callbacks may arrive late,

- retries may duplicate work,

- and users may operate several projects simultaneously.

Architecture must preserve boundaries under failure.

<a id="section-2-50"></a>
## 2.50 Boundary Receipts

Meaningful boundary decisions should produce evidence.

A boundary receipt may record:

- resolved tenant,

- resolved project,

- requesting identity,

- delegated authority,

- data class,

- selected provider account,

- applied policy version,

- budget source,

- approval reference,

- and restrictions applied.

This evidence supports:

- audit,

- incident investigation,

- debugging,

- user explanation,

- and future policy improvement.

Boundary receipts must not expose secrets.

<a id="section-2-51"></a>
## 2.51 Ownership of Errors

The Fabric must assign operational responsibility truthfully.

Possible failure ownership may include:

- caller error,

- policy rejection,

- missing approval,

- budget exhaustion,

- invalid credential,

- provider rejection,

- provider outage,

- adapter defect,

- execution-worker failure,

- output-validation failure,

- asset-storage failure,

- or unknown external outcome.

The system should not report every problem as a generic “AI failure.”

Clear ownership improves recovery and prevents repeated mistakes.

<a id="section-2-52"></a>
## 2.52 Ownership of Generated Outputs

Generated output ownership shall be determined by:

- user ownership,

- tenant and project scope,

- provider terms,

- source rights,

- consent,

- contract,

- and applicable policy.

The model or provider shall not be treated as the owner of the project merely because it produced an output.

The Fabric shall record output ownership claims and restrictions.

It shall not invent legal certainty when terms are ambiguous.

Unclear rights must produce a review requirement.

<a id="section-2-53"></a>
## 2.53 Ownership of Prompts and Production Instructions

Prompts, templates, production instructions and routing policies may be valuable project or platform assets.

The architecture shall distinguish:

- temporary execution prompts,

- reusable project prompts,

- canonical brand instructions,

- provider-specific prompt adaptations,

- system policies,

- and private user instructions.

These objects must be stored and scoped appropriately.

A provider-specific prompt adaptation may be owned by the Fabric adapter.

A Nova and Pling character instruction belongs to Familje-Stunden.

A global data-protection instruction belongs to Omnira governance.

Prompts must not become an unstructured shared pool.

<a id="section-2-54"></a>
## 2.54 Ownership of Provider Performance Evidence

Provider performance evidence may have different scopes.

Platform-level evidence may include:

- availability,

- latency,

- rate-limit behaviour,

- pricing accuracy,

- and adapter reliability.

Project-level evidence may include:

- character consistency,

- brand alignment,

- language quality,

- audience performance,

- or project-specific reviewer scores.

The Fabric shall preserve both.

Project-specific evidence must not automatically become a universal ranking.

A model that performs well for code may perform poorly for children’s animation.

Routing evidence must remain capability-specific and context-aware.

<a id="section-2-55"></a>
## 2.55 Ownership of Derived Learning

Derived learning shall have an explicit owner and scope.

Possible owners include:

- platform,

- tenant,

- project,

- workflow,

- agent,

- or user.

A learning record shall identify:

- evidence,

- derivation method,

- confidence,

- scope,

- creator,

- reviewer,

- date,

- validity period,

- and contradiction state.

No component shall claim permanent ownership of learning merely because it observed the event.

Memory governance determines whether learning persists.

<a id="section-2-56"></a>
## 2.56 Ownership of Fabric Configuration

Fabric configuration shall be versioned and attributable.

Configuration includes:

- provider definitions,

- capability mappings,

- routing profiles,

- quality thresholds,

- budget policies,

- fallback rules,

- data-placement rules,

- and adapter settings.

Changes must record:

- who initiated the change,

- what changed,

- why it changed,

- approval,

- effective time,

- previous version,

- and rollback path.

Configuration drift must not become hidden architecture.

<a id="section-2-57"></a>
## 2.57 Delegation Through the Fabric

A caller may delegate intelligence work.

Delegation does not transfer unlimited authority.

A delegation shall define:

- delegating identity,

- receiving identity,

- task,

- scope,

- capability,

- budget,

- data permissions,

- time limit,

- and escalation path.

The Fabric shall evaluate the delegated authority.

An agent may not further delegate beyond the authority it received unless re-delegation is explicitly permitted.

Delegation chains should remain traceable.

<a id="section-2-58"></a>
## 2.58 The Fabric as an Enforcement Point

The Intelligence Fabric shall act as a policy-enforcement point for governed AI execution.

It may enforce:

- provider allowlists,

- provider denylists,

- model restrictions,

- project isolation,

- data placement,

- capability permissions,

- budget,

- approvals,

- rights requirements,

- quality requirements,

- and retry limits.

The Fabric must not become the only policy source.

Authoritative policies may originate from:

- tenant governance,

- project governance,

- security architecture,

- user settings,

- contracts,

- and approval records.

The Fabric evaluates and applies relevant policy at execution time.

<a id="section-2-59"></a>
## 2.59 The Fabric as a Translation Layer

The Fabric translates between Omnira’s canonical capability language and provider-specific protocols.

Translation may include:

- request format,

- model parameters,

- media formats,

- callback handling,

- error mapping,

- usage reporting,

- and result normalisation.

Translation must preserve meaning.

An adapter shall not silently remove:

- quality requirements,

- privacy constraints,

- commercial-use requirements,

- output format,

- or approval restrictions.

When a provider cannot satisfy a canonical requirement, the adapter must report incompatibility rather than pretending equivalence.

<a id="section-2-60"></a>
## 2.60 The Fabric as an Orchestration Layer

Complex work may require several resources.

The Fabric may orchestrate execution across:

- models,

- tools,

- providers,

- local nodes,

- evaluators,

- and human review.

Orchestration must remain bounded by the caller’s approved objective.

The Fabric may determine execution order and eligible resource selection.

It shall not redefine the project objective.

A production may require:

- one model for script analysis,

- another for image editing,

- another for animation,

- ElevenLabs for narration,

- a local tool for format conversion,

- and a human reviewer for final approval.

The Fabric coordinates the resources.

The project retains ownership of the production.

<a id="section-2-61"></a>
## 2.61 The Fabric as an Economic Control Layer

The Fabric shall provide economic governance for intelligence execution.

It shall help answer:

- What is the expected cost?

- Which account will be charged?

- Which project owns the cost?

- Which lower-cost alternatives exist?

- What quality trade-off would they create?

- What cost has already been incurred?

- How much budget remains?

- Did retries create additional charges?

- Is a premium resource justified?

- Was the final cost within the approved envelope?

Economic control shall support user choice.

It must not hide degradation behind cost savings.

<a id="section-2-62"></a>
## 2.62 The Fabric as a Reliability Layer

The Fabric shall protect callers from unnecessary provider-specific failure complexity.

It may provide:

- standard execution states,

- retry policies,

- fallback rules,

- timeout management,

- idempotency controls,

- callback correlation,

- uncertain-outcome handling,

- and recovery.

The Fabric shall not claim that all providers behave identically.

Provider-specific limitations must remain represented in adapter metadata and execution evidence.

<a id="section-2-63"></a>
## 2.63 The Fabric as a Truth Boundary

The Fabric shall preserve truthful operational state.

It must distinguish:

- requested,

- authorised,

- planned,

- submitted,

- accepted by provider,

- processing,

- output received,

- validated,

- approved,

- completed,

- failed,

- partially completed,

- cancelled,

- and unknown outcome.

A provider’s success response may prove request acceptance, not final output.

A downloaded file may prove output receipt, not quality approval.

A scheduled publication may prove intent, not verified public availability.

Truthful state is a core boundary responsibility.

<a id="section-2-64"></a>
## 2.64 Responsibilities the Fabric Owns

The Intelligence Fabric owns or coordinates the following architectural responsibilities:

1. Canonical capability-request intake.

2. Request scope validation.

3. Authority and policy evaluation.

4. Capability compatibility resolution.

5. Eligible provider and model selection.

6. Provider-account selection.

7. Cost estimation and budget enforcement.

8. Execution planning.

9. Adapter invocation.

10. Asynchronous job coordination.

11. Retry and fallback within policy.

12. Normalised execution state.

13. Provider usage and cost receipts.

14. Execution evidence.

15. Output validation coordination.

16. Provenance creation.

17. Learning-candidate generation.

18. Truthful result reporting.

19. Provider and model lifecycle awareness.

20. Conformance with shared Fabric contracts.

These responsibilities may be implemented through several services.

They remain part of the Fabric architecture.

<a id="section-2-65"></a>
## 2.65 Responsibilities the Fabric Does Not Own

The Intelligence Fabric does not own:

1. The user’s vision.

2. Final business strategy.

3. Executive prioritisation.

4. Project ownership.

5. Permanent agent identity.

6. Long-term memory admission.

7. Full asset-library lifecycle.

8. Public publication authority by default.

9. Repository merge authority.

10. Production deployment authority.

11. Human-resource management.

12. Financial accounting.

13. Legal interpretation.

14. Provider infrastructure.

15. Every application-specific workflow.

16. Unbounded autonomous spending.

17. Independent expansion of permissions.

18. Final human judgement for high-risk outputs.

The Fabric may support these systems.

It must not silently absorb their authority.

<a id="section-2-66"></a>
## 2.66 Prohibited Boundary Collapses

The following boundary collapses are prohibited:

- Atlas directly embedding production provider credentials.

- Project applications bypassing Fabric cost controls.

- Agents storing raw API keys in memory.

- Provider responses being treated as approved memory.

- Generation approval being treated as publication approval.

- A development MCP connection being treated as a production integration.

- Global observability being treated as unrestricted project access.

- Provider availability being treated as provider eligibility.

- Technical possibility being treated as user consent.

- Provider terms being treated as ownership of source assets.

- A model name being treated as an agent identity.

- A successful API call being treated as a verified business outcome.

- An interface filter being treated as architectural tenant isolation.

These failures must be prevented through design, tests and review.

<a id="section-2-67"></a>
## 2.67 System Boundary Invariants

The following invariants shall remain true:

1. Every execution has an identifiable requesting subject.

2. Every project execution has an explicit project scope.

3. Every paid execution has an identifiable budget source.

4. Every provider execution uses an eligible provider account.

5. Every external data transfer respects data classification.

6. Every consequential execution has sufficient authority.

7. Every meaningful output has traceable provenance.

8. Every agent remains distinct from its executing model.

9. Every manual integration is identified as manual or partially automated.

10. Every fallback remains policy-compliant.

11. Every learning remains scoped to appropriate evidence and ownership.

12. Every provider dependency remains visible.

13. Every execution state remains truthful.

14. No component expands its own authority.

15. Missing critical scope fails closed.

These invariants define the minimum architectural integrity of the Fabric.

<a id="section-2-68"></a>
## 2.68 Stage 1 Boundary Requirements

Stage 1 shall establish the boundaries before broad provider coverage.

At minimum, Stage 1 must prove:

- explicit tenant and project scope,

- canonical caller identity,

- capability-based requests,

- policy evaluation,

- provider-account separation,

- credential references rather than raw-secret distribution,

- budget attribution,

- approval boundaries,

- execution receipts,

- provenance,

- and honest execution state.

Stage 1 does not need to automate every workflow.

It must demonstrate that representative integrations obey the same boundaries.

A small number of correctly governed providers is more valuable than a large number of uncontrolled integrations.

<a id="section-2-69"></a>
## 2.69 Conformance Expectations

An implementation conforms to this chapter only when it can demonstrate:

- Fabric ownership is platform-level.

- Atlas is a caller rather than a provider-integration owner.

- project systems cannot access unrelated project data.

- agents remain independent of model identity.

- provider accounts are explicit.

- control-plane changes require appropriate authority.

- data-plane execution follows approved policy.

- evidence is preserved without leaking scope.

- generation and publication authority remain separate.

- manual and automated integration maturity is represented truthfully.

- missing scope fails closed.

- and boundaries are enforced beyond prompt instructions.

Documentation alone is not proof of conformance.

<a id="section-2-70"></a>
## 2.70 Failure Conditions

The architecture shall be considered non-conformant when any of the following conditions exist:

- provider credentials are distributed broadly to agents,

- project scope is inferred from interface branding,

- a missing project identifier causes global access,

- cost cannot be attributed,

- external processing occurs without data-class evaluation,

- an agent’s identity disappears when the model changes,

- provider-specific logic is duplicated throughout applications,

- execution logs automatically become permanent memory,

- publication occurs under generation-only approval,

- or provider integration maturity is overstated.

These conditions require remediation before the affected capability is considered governed.

<a id="section-2-71"></a>
## 2.71 Canonical Ownership Contract

The following requirements define the canonical ownership contract:

1. The Omnira platform SHALL own the Intelligence Fabric architecture.

2. The user SHALL retain ownership of vision and approved direction.

3. Atlas SHALL consume the Fabric through governed requests.

4. Executive Intelligence SHALL own strategic interpretation, not provider execution.

5. Managers SHALL coordinate work, not own provider infrastructure.

6. Agents SHALL own roles within Omnira, not underlying model resources.

7. Providers SHALL supply bounded external capabilities.

8. Projects SHALL own their project-specific context, assets, budgets and policies.

9. Atlas Memory SHALL govern long-term memory admission.

10. Asset systems SHALL govern durable asset lifecycle.

11. Publication systems SHALL govern external publishing actions.

12. Repository systems SHALL govern code-delivery side effects.

13. Credential services SHALL govern raw secrets.

14. Financial systems SHALL remain authoritative for formal accounting.

15. The Fabric SHALL own governed intelligence-resource orchestration.

<a id="section-2-72"></a>
## 2.72 Canonical Boundary Contract

The following requirements define the canonical boundary contract:

1. All governed intelligence execution SHALL pass through the Fabric or an approved equivalent compatibility boundary.

2. Every request SHALL include resolvable identity and scope.

3. Every external provider SHALL remain outside Omnira’s trusted core.

4. Provider access SHALL be mediated by explicit adapters and accounts.

5. Project boundaries SHALL be enforced architecturally.

6. Tenant boundaries SHALL fail closed.

7. Capability authority SHALL be evaluated separately from provider connectivity.

8. Generation authority SHALL remain separate from publication authority.

9. Execution evidence SHALL remain separate from permanent memory.

10. Strategic decisions SHALL remain separate from technical routing.

11. Provider-specific capabilities MAY be supported without provider-specific ownership of the architecture.

12. Development tools MAY support pilots but SHALL NOT become hidden production dependencies.

13. Manual integrations SHALL be represented truthfully.

14. Cross-project execution SHALL require explicit authority.

15. Global scope SHALL never be inferred from missing project scope.

<a id="section-2-73"></a>
## 2.73 Applied Example — Familje-Stunden Production

A future Familje-Stunden production may begin with an approved objective:

Create the September Nova and Pling episode using the approved story, reference images and Swedish narration. Produce one main video, one trailer and five short-form derivatives within the approved budget.

The responsibility chain may be:

1. The user approves the production objective and budget.

2. Atlas interprets the project objective and context.

3. A Media Manager creates the production plan.

4. Specialist agents request required capabilities.

5. The Fabric evaluates project scope, data, rights and authority.

6. The Fabric selects eligible providers and accounts.

7. Provider adapters perform bounded execution.

8. Evaluation services assess character consistency and output quality.

9. A human reviews the final production.

10. The asset system stores approved outputs.

11. A publication service later receives separate authority to publish.

12. Evidence and learning candidates are submitted to their respective systems.

No single provider owns the production.

No agent owns unrestricted credentials.

No successful generation automatically creates publication authority.

The project retains ownership of its approved assets and context.

<a id="section-2-74"></a>
## 2.74 Applied Example — The Prompt News Production

A future The Prompt workflow may request:

Produce a two-minute AI-news segment based on verified current sources, using an approved virtual reporter and low-cost production resources where quality permits.

The responsibility chain may be:

1. Atlas or Executive Intelligence identifies relevant news priorities.

2. A research workflow gathers and verifies sources.

3. The Fabric routes research and summarisation capabilities.

4. A script agent creates a cited draft.

5. A review step verifies accuracy.

6. The Fabric routes avatar, voice, visual and editing tasks.

7. The result is evaluated for factual and brand quality.

8. A human or approved editorial authority accepts the segment.

9. Publication remains a separate governed action.

The Fabric does not decide editorial policy.

The avatar provider does not become the identity of The Prompt.

A cheap video resource may be used only when it satisfies the approved quality standard.

<a id="section-2-75"></a>
## 2.75 Applied Example — GainPilot Content

A future GainPilot workflow may request:

Create a short exercise reel explaining a training principle using approved Arnold branding, a generated still image, narration and motion graphics.

The Fabric may use:

- a local or low-cost language model for the initial script,

- a project-approved image provider,

- ElevenLabs or another eligible voice provider,

- and a lightweight animation tool.

The workflow may cost far less than a Nova and Pling episode because its quality requirements differ.

The shared architecture remains the same.

The project-specific routing policy changes.

<a id="section-2-76"></a>
## 2.76 Applied Example — Omnira Software Work

An Omnira development workflow may request:

Audit the project-scoped media architecture, identify data-isolation defects, propose a corrective plan and implement the approved Stage 1 changes in a protected branch.

The Fabric may provide:

- repository analysis,

- code reasoning,

- test generation,

- and documentation capabilities.

Repository and deployment authority remain separate.

A coding agent may produce a patch.

It may not merge or deploy without the required repository workflow and approval.

<a id="section-2-77"></a>
## 2.77 Future Multi-Tenant Boundary

Omnira’s long-term architecture may support external organisations and customers.

The Intelligence Fabric must therefore be multi-tenant by design even before broad commercial multi-tenancy is activated.

Future tenants may have:

- different providers,

- private credentials,

- custom policies,

- separate budgets,

- regional restrictions,

- project hierarchies,

- legal terms,

- data-retention requirements,

- and approved autonomy.

The Fabric must not require a future rewrite to prevent tenant leakage.

Single-owner operation is not an excuse for global unscoped architecture.

## 2.79 Closing Statement

The Omnira Intelligence Fabric is the shared execution boundary between approved intent and replaceable intelligence resources.

It belongs to the Omnira platform.

Atlas uses it but does not own it.

Executive Intelligence gives strategic meaning to its evidence but does not perform provider routing.

Managers coordinate work but do not control private provider infrastructure.

Agents retain Omnira-owned identities while models remain replaceable.

Projects retain isolated context, assets, budgets and policies.

Providers remain external execution dependencies.

Memory, assets, publication, software delivery and financial accounting retain their own authority boundaries.

The Fabric exists to connect these systems without collapsing them into one uncontrolled intelligence service.

Its power comes from orchestration.

Its safety comes from separation.

Its reliability comes from explicit ownership.

Its scalability comes from shared contracts.

Its legitimacy comes from preserving the user’s authority and the truth of what occurred.
