# Chapter 3 — Doctrine and Architectural Principles

<a id="section-3-1"></a>
## 3.1 Chapter Status

This chapter defines the canonical doctrine and architectural principles of the Omnira Intelligence Fabric.

It establishes the rules that shall govern:

- architecture design,

- implementation decisions,

- provider integration,

- model selection,

- capability routing,

- agent execution,

- cost control,

- data placement,

- evidence collection,

- learning,

- autonomy,

- failure handling,

- and future evolution.

These principles apply across all Fabric components, adapters, providers, projects, environments and implementation stages.

A design that contradicts the doctrine of this chapter shall not be considered conformant merely because it works technically.

Technical feasibility does not override architecture.

Convenience does not override authority.

Provider functionality does not override user ownership.

Cost reduction does not override required quality, privacy, rights or safety.

This chapter is normative after formal approval.

It does not prove that the current runtime already enforces every principle.

Implementation and conformance must be verified separately.

<a id="section-3-2"></a>
## 3.2 Purpose of Architectural Doctrine

The Intelligence Fabric will operate across a rapidly changing ecosystem.

Providers will appear and disappear.

Model names will change.

Pricing will change.

Capabilities will improve.

Terms of service will evolve.

New protocols will emerge.

Some integrations will become obsolete.

Some providers will become strategically important.

Others will become too expensive, unreliable or restrictive.

A stable architecture cannot depend on permanent external conditions.

The Fabric therefore requires a doctrine that remains valid even when individual technologies change.

The doctrine defines how Omnira shall reason about intelligence execution regardless of:

- provider,

- model,

- protocol,

- modality,

- location,

- deployment environment,

- pricing model,

- or implementation language.

The doctrine is the continuity layer of the architecture.

Implementation may evolve.

The doctrine must remain deliberate.

<a id="section-3-3"></a>
## 3.3 Doctrine Hierarchy

The Intelligence Fabric shall apply its principles through a clear hierarchy.

When principles appear to compete, the following precedence shall normally apply:

1. Human legitimacy and lawful authority.

2. Tenant and project isolation.

3. Security and data protection.

4. Rights, consent and contractual restrictions.

5. Truthful representation of system state.

6. Capability compatibility and required quality.

7. Reliability and recoverability.

8. Budget and economic constraints.

9. Latency and operational efficiency.

10. User preference and optimisation profile.

This hierarchy does not mean that lower-ranked concerns are unimportant.

It means that a cheaper or faster result must not override a more fundamental requirement.

For example:

- Economy routing must not send confidential data to an unapproved provider.

- Premium routing must not exceed the approved budget.

- Pinned routing must not use an unavailable or prohibited provider.

- A fast result must not be represented as completed when validation is still pending.

- A technically capable provider must not be used when commercial rights are insufficient.

Conflicts shall be resolved explicitly rather than silently.

<a id="section-3-4"></a>
## 3.4 Principle — No Intelligence Without Fabric

All governed artificial intelligence execution within Omnira shall pass through the Omnira Intelligence Fabric or through an explicitly approved compatibility boundary that enforces equivalent controls.

This principle is known as:

No Intelligence Without Fabric.

It exists to prevent intelligence execution from becoming fragmented across:

- application code,

- isolated scripts,

- agent prompts,

- provider dashboards,

- hidden API calls,

- browser automation,

- and ungoverned development tools.

The principle requires that governed execution includes:

- identity,

- scope,

- authority,

- policy,

- provider eligibility,

- cost attribution,

- execution state,

- evidence,

- and output provenance.

Temporary experiments may operate outside the production Fabric.

They must be declared as experimental and must not be represented as governed production capability.

The principle does not prohibit innovation.

It prevents innovation from silently bypassing governance.

<a id="section-3-5"></a>
## 3.5 Principle — Human Vision Remains Primary

The Fabric exists to help users realise their intentions.

It does not own the intention.

It does not determine what a person should value.

It does not replace human responsibility for consequential direction.

Human users may delegate bounded work.

They may authorise recurring production.

They may permit low-risk autonomous execution.

They may rely on Atlas and Executive Intelligence for recommendations.

The source of legitimate direction remains human authority or an explicitly authorised organisational governance process.

The Fabric shall never treat technical capability as evidence that an action should occur.

It shall distinguish:

- can be done,

- may be done,

- should be done,

- has been approved,

- and has been completed.

These states are not equivalent.

<a id="section-3-6"></a>
## 3.6 Principle — Intent Before Implementation

Callers shall express the desired outcome before selecting implementation details.

A capability request shall begin with:

- objective,

- required result,

- constraints,

- context,

- quality,

- authority,

- cost,

- timing,

- and data requirements.

Provider and model selection shall follow from the approved request.

A caller may pin a provider or model when there is a legitimate reason.

Pinned execution is an explicit exception, not the default architecture.

This principle prevents provider names from becoming substitutes for requirements.

The question shall not begin with:

Which provider should we use?

The question shall begin with:

What must be achieved, under which conditions, and what resources are eligible to achieve it?

<a id="section-3-7"></a>
## 3.7 Principle — Capability First

The Fabric shall model intelligence in terms of capabilities.

A capability describes an outcome or operation that a resource can perform.

Examples include:

- reason over a long document,

- generate production-quality code,

- verify current sources,

- create Swedish speech,

- animate an approved illustration,

- preserve character consistency,

- edit text inside an image,

- transcribe audio,

- generate embeddings,

- rerank evidence,

- or evaluate a video against brand requirements.

Providers and models implement capabilities.

They do not define the canonical language of Omnira’s business logic.

Capability-first design allows the Fabric to:

- compare resources,

- replace providers,

- combine models,

- enforce policy consistently,

- and adopt future technologies without rewriting callers.

A capability definition must be precise enough to distinguish meaningful differences.

“Generate media” is too broad.

“Generate a 16:9 image-to-video scene using approved character references with commercial-use eligibility” is materially more useful.

<a id="section-3-8"></a>
## 3.8 Principle — Providers Are Replaceable Resources

Providers shall be treated as replaceable execution resources.

A provider may offer valuable technology, unique capabilities or favourable economics.

The Fabric may rely on a provider for a bounded period or capability.

The architecture must still preserve:

- dependency visibility,

- account separation,

- provider lifecycle,

- data-placement awareness,

- cost attribution,

- fallback planning,

- and an exit strategy.

Replaceability does not mean instant substitution is always possible.

Some providers may expose unique capabilities that cannot be reproduced elsewhere.

In those cases, the dependency shall be explicit.

Hidden lock-in is prohibited.

Deliberate dependency is permitted when documented, approved and monitored.

<a id="section-3-9"></a>
## 3.9 Principle — Models Are Not Agents

A model is an execution resource.

An agent is an Omnira-governed identity.

An agent may use several models for different tasks.

Several agents may use the same model.

A model change shall not automatically change:

- agent name,

- role,

- memory,

- authority,

- project relationships,

- behavioural policy,

- or lifecycle.

An external provider may describe a model-backed feature as an agent.

Omnira shall still evaluate it as an external resource unless it has been deliberately admitted into Omnira’s agent architecture.

The distinction protects continuity and prevents external vendors from owning the identity of Omnira’s workforce.

<a id="section-3-10"></a>
## 3.10 Principle — Omnira Owns Context and Authority

Project context, memory, identity and authority shall remain governed by Omnira.

Providers may receive the minimum context needed to execute an approved task.

They shall not become the canonical owners of:

- project history,

- strategic memory,

- agent identity,

- user preferences,

- approval state,

- policy,

- or execution authority.

Provider-hosted threads, workspaces and assistants may be useful.

They shall not silently become the system of record for Omnira.

Where provider-side state is used, Omnira shall record:

- what state exists,

- why it is used,

- what scope it contains,

- how it is synchronised,

- how it can be deleted,

- and what happens if the provider becomes unavailable.

<a id="section-3-11"></a>
## 3.11 Principle — Truth Before Appearance

The Fabric shall represent what actually occurred.

It shall not optimise user experience by presenting uncertain or partial states as success.

The following distinctions must remain visible:

- request created,

- request authorised,

- execution planned,

- provider call submitted,

- provider call accepted,

- output generated,

- output received,

- output validated,

- output approved,

- output stored,

- output published,

- and output verified live.

A clean interface does not justify false certainty.

The system must prefer an honest “unknown outcome” over an unsupported “failed” or “completed.”

Truthful state is a functional requirement.

<a id="section-3-12"></a>
## 3.12 Principle — Fail Closed on Missing Critical Context

When required identity, scope, authority, classification, rights or budget cannot be resolved, the Fabric shall fail closed.

It shall not infer broad authority from missing data.

It shall not fall back to:

- global scope,

- a default project,

- a personal provider account,

- an unrestricted credential,

- a public model,

- or an unlimited budget.

Examples of fail-closed conditions include:

- missing tenant,

- unresolved project,

- unknown requester,

- expired approval,

- absent data classification,

- unclear voice consent,

- unverified commercial rights,

- missing credential ownership,

- and unavailable budget source.

The system shall report why execution was blocked and what is required to proceed.

<a id="section-3-13"></a>
## 3.13 Principle — Least Privilege

Every caller, agent, workflow, adapter and provider account shall receive only the access required for its approved role.

Least privilege applies to:

- capabilities,

- data,

- tools,

- credentials,

- projects,

- provider accounts,

- budgets,

- publication actions,

- administrative actions,

- and retention.

An image-generation agent does not require permission to delete provider assets.

A research workflow does not require access to private voice-cloning tools.

A project agent does not require global tenant credentials.

A callback worker does not require authority to change routing policy.

Permissions shall be narrow, explicit and revocable.

<a id="section-3-14"></a>
## 3.14 Principle — Scope Before Execution

Every execution shall resolve its scope before provider selection.

Scope may include:

- tenant,

- user,

- project,

- environment,

- agent,

- workflow,

- production,

- capability,

- and purpose.

Scope must propagate through:

- policy evaluation,

- provider selection,

- budget,

- execution,

- evidence,

- outputs,

- memory candidates,

- and incident handling.

A scope shall not be inferred from branding, filenames or user-interface location alone.

Scope must come from an authoritative object or identity relationship.

Missing scope is an execution error.

<a id="section-3-15"></a>
## 3.15 Principle — Shared Infrastructure, Isolated Context

The Fabric may use shared platform infrastructure.

It shall not create ungoverned shared context.

Shared components may include:

- registries,

- routing services,

- execution workers,

- observability systems,

- adapter libraries,

- and health monitoring.

Project and tenant information must remain isolated within those systems.

The architecture shall support global operational insight without granting global content access.

A shared worker may process tasks for several projects.

Each task must remain independently scoped, authorised and evidenced.

<a id="section-3-16"></a>
## 3.16 Principle — Data Minimisation

The Fabric shall send providers only the information necessary to complete the approved task.

More context is not always better.

Unnecessary context increases:

- privacy risk,

- cost,

- latency,

- prompt complexity,

- accidental disclosure,

- and provider dependence.

Data minimisation may include:

- retrieval filtering,

- field selection,

- redaction,

- pseudonymisation,

- summarisation,

- local preprocessing,

- reference substitution,

- and scoped asset access.

The Fabric shall preserve enough context for quality while avoiding indiscriminate context transfer.

<a id="section-3-17"></a>
## 3.17 Principle — Classification Determines Placement

Data classification shall constrain where work may execute.

The Fabric shall not route first and evaluate data restrictions afterward.

Workload placement must be determined before protected data is transmitted.

Expected classifications include:

- Public,

- Project Internal,

- Confidential,

- Local Only,

- and Prohibited.

Classification may apply to:

- prompts,

- documents,

- images,

- voices,

- credentials,

- metadata,

- memories,

- outputs,

- and derived evidence.

Derived output may inherit or exceed the classification of its inputs.

Transformation does not automatically remove sensitivity.

<a id="section-3-18"></a>
## 3.18 Principle — Protection May Increase, Never Silently Decrease

The Fabric may increase protection when risk is detected.

For example:

- Project Internal data may be treated as Confidential when sensitive personal information is discovered.

- A public asset may receive restricted provenance because it was created from confidential sources.

- A workflow may be moved from external execution to local execution after policy evaluation.

The Fabric shall not silently lower protection.

A lower classification requires:

- explicit policy,

- authorised review,

- valid transformation,

- or owner approval.

The reason for reclassification must be recorded.

<a id="section-3-19"></a>
## 3.19 Principle — Rights Before Execution

Technical eligibility is not sufficient.

The Fabric shall verify applicable rights and consent before consequential generation or publication preparation.

Relevant concerns may include:

- copyright,

- trademark,

- commercial-use rights,

- voice consent,

- likeness consent,

- style restrictions,

- source licences,

- provider terms,

- territorial restrictions,

- and project ownership.

A provider stating that commercial output is allowed does not prove that the inputs were lawfully supplied.

The Fabric must evaluate the complete rights chain.

Unclear rights shall produce a review requirement.

<a id="section-3-20"></a>
## 3.20 Principle — Quality Is a Contract

Quality shall be defined as an explicit task requirement.

The Fabric shall not rely only on general provider reputation.

A quality contract may specify:

- factual accuracy,

- visual consistency,

- brand alignment,

- code correctness,

- source quality,

- child safety,

- voice naturalness,

- format compliance,

- resolution,

- latency,

- or acceptance criteria.

The quality contract determines:

- eligible resources,

- evaluation method,

- retry policy,

- human-review requirements,

- and completion state.

A generated result is not accepted merely because the provider returned a file.

<a id="section-3-21"></a>
## 3.21 Principle — Quality Is Contextual

There is no universally best model.

A model may perform well for:

- code,

- animation,

- Swedish speech,

- visual typography,

- or research.

It may perform poorly for another capability.

Provider evidence shall therefore be:

- capability-specific,

- task-specific where necessary,

- version-aware,

- time-aware,

- and context-aware.

A strong result in one project does not automatically prove global superiority.

The Fabric shall avoid simplistic universal rankings.

<a id="section-3-22"></a>
## 3.22 Principle — Cheapest Eligible, Not Cheapest Available

Economic routing shall select the least expensive eligible path that can satisfy the approved requirements.

The cheapest available model may be ineligible because of:

- insufficient quality,

- incompatible capability,

- privacy restrictions,

- poor reliability,

- missing commercial rights,

- unacceptable latency,

- or excessive correction cost.

Total production cost shall include:

- provider charges,

- failed attempts,

- retries,

- human correction,

- post-processing,

- delay,

- and unusable output.

A higher-priced execution may be economically superior when it reliably produces an acceptable result.

<a id="section-3-23"></a>
## 3.23 Principle — Cost Must Be Visible

The Fabric shall make intelligence cost observable.

Where possible, it shall record:

- estimated cost,

- reserved budget,

- actual provider usage,

- actual cost,

- currency,

- credits,

- project attribution,

- account attribution,

- retries,

- variance,

- and unconfirmed charges.

Estimated and confirmed cost must remain distinct.

The system must not imply exact cost when provider pricing or usage reporting is incomplete.

Cost visibility applies to:

- user-owned accounts,

- Omnira-managed resources,

- local execution,

- free tiers,

- and bundled subscriptions.

Zero incremental provider charge does not mean zero operational cost.

<a id="section-3-24"></a>
## 3.24 Principle — No Hidden Cross-Subsidisation

The Fabric shall not silently use one project’s credits or provider accounts to fund another project.

Shared accounts may be permitted.

Cost attribution must still remain explicit.

A project may use a platform-wide budget only when policy allows it.

The system shall not automatically fall back from:

- project credits,

- to personal credits,

- to Omnira credits,

- or to another tenant’s capacity.

Payment-path fallback requires explicit policy or approval.

<a id="section-3-25"></a>
## 3.25 Principle — Bounded Autonomy

Autonomy shall operate only inside a defined envelope.

An autonomy envelope may specify:

- scope,

- objective,

- capabilities,

- providers,

- budget,

- retry limit,

- duration,

- output requirements,

- data classes,

- and prohibited actions.

The Fabric may choose eligible implementation details within the envelope.

It must stop when the envelope is exceeded or becomes invalid.

Autonomy must be:

- explicit,

- observable,

- revocable,

- attributable,

- and unable to expand itself.

There is no implied unlimited autonomy.

<a id="section-3-26"></a>
## 3.26 Principle — Approval Is Specific

An approval shall authorise only the action and scope it describes.

Approval to generate does not automatically authorise:

- publication,

- deletion,

- account changes,

- increased budget,

- provider substitution,

- voice cloning,

- rights acceptance,

- or cross-project access.

Approvals shall identify:

- approver,

- subject,

- action,

- scope,

- constraints,

- validity period,

- and status.

A stale or ambiguous approval must not be reused.

Approval inheritance must be explicit.

<a id="section-3-27"></a>
## 3.27 Principle — Delegation Does Not Create New Authority

An agent or Manager may delegate work only within the authority it received.

Delegation shall not create permissions that the delegating subject did not possess.

A delegation chain must remain traceable.

Re-delegation may be allowed only when explicitly permitted.

Delegated authority should normally be narrower than or equal to the original authority.

The Fabric shall evaluate both:

- the current requester,

- and the originating authority chain.

A trusted agent must not become an unrestricted authority broker.

<a id="section-3-28"></a>
## 3.28 Principle — Providers Do Not Receive Business Authority

Providers execute bounded technical requests.

They do not receive independent authority to:

- change project direction,

- create new tasks,

- increase spend,

- publish content,

- contact users,

- modify unrelated assets,

- or select additional providers.

A provider-hosted agent may perform internal reasoning.

Its authority remains limited to the submitted execution.

Any external side effect requires explicit tool and action permission.

<a id="section-3-29"></a>
## 3.29 Principle — Evidence by Default

Meaningful Fabric execution shall produce evidence.

Evidence may include:

- request,

- policy decision,

- selected resource,

- provider account,

- execution state,

- usage,

- cost,

- outputs,

- evaluations,

- errors,

- fallback,

- approvals,

- and completion status.

Evidence shall be sufficient to answer:

- What happened?

- Why was it allowed?

- What resource was used?

- What did it cost?

- What result was produced?

- How was the result evaluated?

- What remains uncertain?

Evidence must remain proportionate.

Temporary low-risk operations need not produce the same detail as commercial media, confidential analysis or consequential software changes.

<a id="section-3-30"></a>
## 3.30 Principle — Provenance Follows the Output

Meaningful outputs shall retain provenance as they move between systems.

Provenance should not disappear when an output is:

- renamed,

- edited,

- converted,

- exported,

- published,

- embedded,

- or reused.

Derivative assets shall preserve links to their sources.

A social-media clip derived from a Nova and Pling episode should remain connected to:

- the master episode,

- approved character assets,

- narration source,

- generation execution,

- and publication history.

Provenance supports ownership, rights, quality, reuse and incident response.

<a id="section-3-31"></a>
## 3.31 Principle — Logs Are Not Memory

Execution logs shall not automatically become long-term memory.

Logs describe events.

Memory represents governed information intended for future retrieval and reasoning.

A raw provider response may contain:

- errors,

- speculation,

- temporary values,

- sensitive content,

- or irrelevant detail.

Directly promoting all logs into memory would create:

- noise,

- contradiction,

- privacy risk,

- and false learning.

The Fabric shall create memory candidates only when evidence supports a useful, scoped and governable learning.

<a id="section-3-32"></a>
## 3.32 Principle — Learning Requires Evidence

Derived learning shall be supported by identifiable evidence.

A learning record should include:

- claim,

- source executions,

- relevant conditions,

- scope,

- confidence,

- date,

- reviewer where required,

- validity period,

- and contradiction state.

A single successful generation may suggest a hypothesis.

It may not justify a permanent platform-wide rule.

Learning shall reflect the strength of its evidence.

<a id="section-3-33"></a>
## 3.33 Principle — Learning Must Remain Scoped

A lesson from one context must not be applied universally without justification.

Examples:

- Nova and Pling character consistency evidence belongs primarily to Familje-Stunden and the relevant video capability.

- GainPilot short-form performance belongs to GainPilot’s audience and channel context.

- General provider outage evidence may be platform-wide.

- A customer-specific preference must remain tenant-specific.

- Confidential production learning must not enter public routing evidence.

Learning scope shall be explicit.

<a id="section-3-34"></a>
## 3.34 Principle — Learning May Be Reversed

Fabric learning shall be revisable.

Providers update models.

Pricing changes.

Performance shifts.

Project needs evolve.

A previous learning may become:

- outdated,

- contradicted,

- superseded,

- or invalid.

Learning records must support:

- review,

- expiry,

- supersession,

- contradiction,

- and deletion.

The Fabric shall not treat historical evidence as permanent truth.

<a id="section-3-35"></a>
## 3.35 Principle — Reversibility Where Possible

Architecture and execution should preserve rollback and recovery where technically possible.

Reversible actions are safer to automate than irreversible actions.

Examples include:

- generating a draft,

- creating a new asset version,

- reserving rather than immediately consuming budget,

- staging configuration,

- and saving a proposed routing rule.

Higher scrutiny is required for:

- deletion,

- overwriting canonical assets,

- public publication,

- external communication,

- credential revocation,

- financial commitment,

- and irreversible provider operations.

The Fabric should prefer additive and versioned changes over destructive mutation.

<a id="section-3-36"></a>
## 3.36 Principle — Idempotency Must Be Designed

Retries are unavoidable.

The Fabric shall design idempotency for operations where duplicate execution would create risk or cost.

Idempotency may require:

- stable request identifiers,

- provider idempotency keys,

- execution deduplication,

- callback deduplication,

- asset correlation,

- budget reservation tracking,

- and completion receipts.

The Fabric shall not assume that retrying a timed-out generation is harmless.

A timeout may occur after the provider accepted the request.

Blind retries may create duplicate assets and charges.

<a id="section-3-37"></a>
## 3.37 Principle — Unknown Outcome Is a Valid State

When the Fabric cannot determine whether an external operation completed, it shall use an explicit unknown-outcome state.

Unknown outcome may occur when:

- a provider times out after accepting a request,

- a callback is lost,

- a network connection closes,

- a publication response is ambiguous,

- or provider status cannot be retrieved.

The system shall not automatically classify unknown outcome as failure.

Recovery may require:

- provider reconciliation,

- account inspection,

- delayed callback processing,

- human verification,

- or safe compensation.

Truth is more important than premature closure.

<a id="section-3-38"></a>
## 3.38 Principle — Retries Must Be Bounded

Retries shall follow explicit policy.

Retry policy should consider:

- operation cost,

- idempotency,

- failure category,

- provider guidance,

- remaining budget,

- deadline,

- and likelihood of recovery.

The Fabric shall not retry indefinitely.

It shall not retry permanent policy rejection.

It shall not retry an expensive non-idempotent operation without evidence that the first attempt failed safely.

Retry exhaustion must produce a truthful terminal or escalation state.

<a id="section-3-39"></a>
## 3.39 Principle — Fallback Is Not a Policy Escape

Fallback shall select another eligible execution path.

It shall not weaken:

- data protection,

- rights requirements,

- quality threshold,

- budget,

- project scope,

- or authority.

A Local Only request may not fall back to a public cloud provider.

A provider pinned for contractual reasons may not be replaced silently.

A premium-quality request may not fall back to a materially lower-quality model without approval.

Fallback eligibility must be evaluated under the original request’s constraints.

<a id="section-3-40"></a>
## 3.40 Principle — Graceful Degradation Must Be Explicit

When the complete requested outcome cannot be achieved, the Fabric may propose a reduced result.

Examples include:

- lower resolution,

- fewer variations,

- delayed completion,

- manual review,

- partial production,

- or use of an alternative format.

Degradation requires:

- clear disclosure,

- continued compliance,

- and approval when the change is material.

The system shall not silently deliver a lower standard while claiming full completion.

<a id="section-3-41"></a>
## 3.41 Principle — Local and Private Resources Are First-Class

Local, self-hosted and private resources shall be represented as first-class Fabric resources.

They shall not be treated merely as emergency fallbacks.

Local execution may offer:

- privacy,

- cost control,

- offline capability,

- predictable availability,

- and user ownership.

Local resources also have limitations such as:

- hardware capacity,

- energy use,

- model size,

- maintenance,

- slower execution,

- and weaker capability.

The Fabric shall evaluate local resources using the same truthful capability and quality contracts as external providers.

<a id="section-3-42"></a>
## 3.42 Principle — Location Is Part of Eligibility

Execution location shall influence resource eligibility.

Relevant locations may include:

- user device,

- private local rig,

- tenant server,

- Omnira-managed infrastructure,

- regional cloud,

- or external provider.

Location decisions shall consider:

- data classification,

- latency,

- hardware,

- cost,

- reliability,

- legal region,

- and provider terms.

The Fabric must not describe a resource as private merely because it uses an encrypted external API.

Private, local and external are distinct deployment properties.

<a id="section-3-43"></a>
## 3.43 Principle — Multimodal Capabilities Share Governance

Text, image, voice, audio, music and video shall use a common governance model.

The architecture may have modality-specific execution logic.

It shall not have weaker policy merely because an output is media rather than text.

A video generation requires the same categories of concern:

- identity,

- scope,

- authority,

- cost,

- rights,

- evidence,

- provider eligibility,

- and output handling.

Voice and likeness may require stronger consent controls.

The modality changes the requirements.

It does not remove governance.

<a id="section-3-44"></a>
## 3.44 Principle — Provider-Specific Strengths Stay Isolated

The Fabric may use provider-specific features.

Such features shall be isolated in:

- adapters,

- capability extensions,

- provider metadata,

- or explicitly pinned workflows.

Provider-specific concepts shall not spread across unrelated application logic.

For example, an OpenArt-specific character reference feature may be valuable.

Familje-Stunden should request character-consistent animation.

The OpenArt adapter may translate that requirement into provider-specific parameters.

The project workflow should not become permanently dependent on OpenArt parameter names.

<a id="section-3-45"></a>
## 3.45 Principle — Adapters Are Anti-Corruption Boundaries

Provider adapters shall protect Omnira’s canonical architecture from provider-specific inconsistency.

An adapter may translate:

- request fields,

- authentication,

- model names,

- media formats,

- parameters,

- errors,

- callbacks,

- usage,

- and output metadata.

The adapter shall not hide material provider limitations.

It must report when canonical requirements cannot be satisfied.

Adapters shall not fabricate equivalence.

They shall preserve meaning while isolating external complexity.

<a id="section-3-46"></a>
## 3.46 Principle — Protocol Does Not Define Trust

API, MCP, webhook and local execution are transport or integration mechanisms.

They do not determine whether an integration is trusted.

An MCP server may expose powerful and destructive tools.

A traditional API may be narrowly scoped and safe.

A local tool may still access sensitive data or damage files.

Each integration shall be evaluated according to:

- identity,

- permissions,

- behaviour,

- data handling,

- reversibility,

- evidence,

- and operational maturity.

Protocol convenience must not be confused with safety.

<a id="section-3-47"></a>
## 3.47 Principle — Manual Work Must Be Represented Honestly

A partially manual workflow may be useful and legitimate.

The Fabric shall distinguish:

- fully automated,

- API automated,

- MCP automated,

- partially automated,

- human-assisted,

- manual external,

- experimental,

- and unavailable.

A human-assisted OpenArt production may still be governed through:

- approved inputs,

- budget,

- instructions,

- output ingestion,

- provenance,

- and review.

The system must not claim autonomous completion when a person must perform the central action.

<a id="section-3-48"></a>
## 3.48 Principle — Experimental Capability Is Not Production Capability

Experiments are necessary.

They shall remain isolated from production authority.

Experimental integrations may use:

- temporary credentials,

- test accounts,

- manual workflows,

- limited data,

- or development clients.

Before production admission, the integration must demonstrate:

- stable identity,

- secure authentication,

- policy enforcement,

- cost handling,

- failure behaviour,

- evidence,

- isolation,

- and supportability.

Successful experimentation is evidence.

It is not production certification.

<a id="section-3-49"></a>
## 3.49 Principle — Simulation Must Never Be Confused With Execution

The Fabric may simulate:

- routing,

- cost,

- provider selection,

- failure,

- latency,

- or execution plans.

Simulation is valuable for:

- testing,

- forecasting,

- policy evaluation,

- and approval preparation.

A simulated result shall not be represented as:

- provider execution,

- real cost,

- actual output,

- verified success,

- or completed business action.

Simulation records must be clearly marked.

<a id="section-3-50"></a>
## 3.50 Principle — Version Everything That Affects Meaning

Meaningful Fabric objects shall be version-aware.

This includes:

- capability definitions,

- provider definitions,

- model definitions,

- adapters,

- policies,

- routing profiles,

- quality contracts,

- prompts,

- schemas,

- approvals,

- and evaluation methods.

An execution receipt should identify the versions that affected the result.

Without versioning, the same request may produce materially different behaviour without explanation.

Versioning supports:

- audit,

- rollback,

- reproduction,

- migration,

- and learning.

<a id="section-3-51"></a>
## 3.51 Principle — Stable Core, Evolvable Edges

The Fabric shall preserve stable canonical contracts while allowing provider-specific and technological change at the edges.

Stable core concepts include:

- capability request,

- identity,

- scope,

- policy,

- provider,

- model,

- execution,

- evidence,

- cost,

- output,

- evaluation,

- and approval.

Provider adapters, model versions and operational strategies may evolve more frequently.

The architecture should minimise how often core callers must change when providers change.

<a id="section-3-52"></a>
## 3.52 Principle — No Hidden Global Defaults

Defaults may simplify use.

They must not create hidden authority or scope.

Safe defaults may include:

- Balanced routing,

- no publication authority,

- bounded retries,

- deny on missing scope,

- and manual review for high-risk output.

Unsafe defaults include:

- global project access,

- unrestricted provider account,

- unlimited spending,

- public data classification,

- automatic memory admission,

- and automatic publication.

Defaults must be explicit, documented and versioned.

<a id="section-3-53"></a>
## 3.53 Principle — Configuration Is Governed State

Fabric configuration shall be treated as governed operational state.

Configuration changes may affect:

- cost,

- privacy,

- provider choice,

- quality,

- and authority.

Changes shall be:

- attributable,

- versioned,

- reviewable,

- testable,

- and reversible where possible.

A configuration change shall not be hidden inside a prompt or undocumented environment variable when it materially changes system behaviour.

<a id="section-3-54"></a>
## 3.54 Principle — Secrets Never Belong in Prompts

Raw credentials and secrets shall not be placed in:

- prompts,

- agent memory,

- logs,

- generated documents,

- asset metadata,

- user-facing errors,

- or repository files.

Callers and agents should use credential references or governed connection identifiers.

Secret access shall occur only at the execution boundary that requires it.

The system shall minimise the number of components that can access raw secrets.

<a id="section-3-55"></a>
## 3.55 Principle — Provider Accounts Are Explicit Resources

A provider and a provider account are separate objects.

The same provider may be available through:

- personal account,

- project account,

- tenant account,

- production account,

- sandbox account,

- or Omnira-managed account.

The Fabric shall select an eligible account, not merely an eligible provider.

Account selection affects:

- cost,

- credits,

- privacy,

- assets,

- region,

- rights,

- and authority.

Silent account substitution is prohibited.

<a id="section-3-56"></a>
## 3.56 Principle — Provider Health Does Not Equal Provider Eligibility

A provider may be technically healthy but ineligible for a particular request.

A provider may be eligible but temporarily unhealthy.

The Fabric shall distinguish:

- capability eligibility,

- policy eligibility,

- account eligibility,

- health,

- capacity,

- and current availability.

Routing must satisfy all required conditions.

Health monitoring alone cannot determine execution permission.

<a id="section-3-57"></a>
## 3.57 Principle — Deprecation Is a Managed Lifecycle

Models, providers, adapters and capabilities shall have explicit lifecycle states.

Expected states may include:

- proposed,

- experimental,

- approved,

- active,

- restricted,

- degraded,

- deprecated,

- disabled,

- and retired.

Deprecation shall include:

- reason,

- effective date,

- affected workflows,

- migration path,

- fallback,

- and evidence preservation.

A model shall not disappear from the registry merely because it is no longer selectable.

Historical executions must remain interpretable.

<a id="section-3-58"></a>
## 3.58 Principle — Policy Is Evaluated at Execution Time

Policies may change after a workflow is created.

The Fabric shall evaluate relevant policy when execution occurs.

Long-running jobs may also require policy revalidation at significant boundaries.

An old workflow definition shall not preserve outdated permission indefinitely.

Execution-time policy evaluation must still respect approved production envelopes and stable commitments.

Material policy changes should produce explicit interruption or reapproval rather than silent behavioural change.

<a id="section-3-59"></a>
## 3.59 Principle — Policy Decisions Are Evidenced

The Fabric should record material policy decisions.

A policy-decision record may identify:

- policy version,

- input scope,

- classification,

- eligible providers,

- blocked providers,

- budget result,

- approval result,

- and final decision.

This allows the system to explain why:

- a provider was selected,

- a request was blocked,

- a fallback was denied,

- or a local execution was required.

Explainability shall not expose secrets or sensitive internal details unnecessarily.

<a id="section-3-60"></a>
## 3.60 Principle — Non-Determinism Must Be Acknowledged

Many AI systems are non-deterministic.

The same request may produce different outputs.

The Fabric shall not promise exact reproducibility when the provider or model cannot provide it.

Where practical, execution records may preserve:

- model version,

- seed,

- temperature,

- generation settings,

- inputs,

- and provider job identifiers.

Reproduction may mean recreating equivalent conditions, not guaranteeing identical output.

The system shall state the difference honestly.

<a id="section-3-61"></a>
## 3.61 Principle — Evaluation Must Be Independent Where Risk Requires It

The same model that generated an output should not always be the sole authority evaluating it.

Higher-risk work may require:

- deterministic validation,

- another model,

- a specialist reviewer,

- human approval,

- or multiple evaluation methods.

Examples include:

- factual news,

- security-sensitive code,

- child-facing media,

- commercial rights,

- and major business recommendations.

Evaluation independence should increase with consequence and uncertainty.

<a id="section-3-62"></a>
## 3.62 Principle — Completion Requires Acceptance Criteria

An execution may be technically complete without satisfying the request.

The Fabric shall distinguish:

- execution completion,

- output validation,

- quality acceptance,

- human approval,

- and external outcome verification.

A task may close only according to its defined completion contract.

For example:

- a video job is not accepted merely because a file exists;

- code is not accepted merely because text was generated;

- research is not accepted without required sources;

- publication is not complete until verified live when verification is required.

<a id="section-3-63"></a>
## 3.63 Principle — Errors Must Preserve Meaning

Provider-specific errors shall be normalised without losing relevant detail.

Canonical error categories may include:

- invalid request,

- policy rejection,

- missing authority,

- budget rejection,

- authentication failure,

- rate limit,

- provider outage,

- unsupported capability,

- rights restriction,

- safety rejection,

- timeout,

- unknown outcome,

- validation failure,

- and internal defect.

A generic “AI error” is insufficient for governed recovery.

The original provider evidence should remain available within appropriate access controls.

<a id="section-3-64"></a>
## 3.64 Principle — Observability Must Respect Privacy

Observability shall not become a shadow data store.

Metrics, logs and traces must follow:

- tenant isolation,

- project isolation,

- data minimisation,

- retention,

- redaction,

- and access control.

Sensitive prompts and assets should not be copied into broad operational logs.

The Fabric must preserve enough evidence for audit without exposing unnecessary content.

<a id="section-3-65"></a>
## 3.65 Principle — Architecture Must Survive Agent Failure

The Fabric shall not depend on every agent interpreting instructions perfectly.

Agents may:

- misunderstand,

- hallucinate,

- request excessive context,

- choose unsuitable tools,

- or attempt actions outside their role.

Architectural controls must enforce:

- scope,

- authority,

- cost,

- provider eligibility,

- and data placement.

Prompt instructions support safe behaviour.

They do not replace enforceable boundaries.

<a id="section-3-66"></a>
## 3.66 Principle — Architecture Must Survive Provider Change

Providers may alter:

- model behaviour,

- APIs,

- pricing,

- limits,

- moderation,

- output formats,

- and terms.

The Fabric shall detect and manage material change through:

- version monitoring,

- adapter tests,

- health checks,

- capability re-evaluation,

- policy review,

- and controlled rollout.

Provider change must not silently redefine Omnira’s contracts.

<a id="section-3-67"></a>
## 3.67 Principle — Architecture Must Survive Partial Failure

Complex production may succeed only in part.

The Fabric shall support partial state.

A workflow may complete:

- script,

- narration,

- and eight scenes,

while two scenes fail.

The system shall preserve successful outputs where safe.

It shall not discard useful work merely because the full production is incomplete.

It shall also not represent the complete production as successful.

Partial completion requires explicit reconciliation.

<a id="section-3-68"></a>
## 3.68 Principle — Architecture Must Support Human Recovery

Human operators shall be able to understand and recover failed or uncertain execution.

The Fabric should provide:

- clear status,

- execution history,

- failed step,

- cost incurred,

- remaining budget,

- available outputs,

- retry options,

- fallback options,

- and required approval.

Recovery must not require direct database manipulation or provider guesswork for ordinary cases.

<a id="section-3-69"></a>
## 3.69 Principle — Security Boundaries Are Architectural

Security shall not depend only on:

- model obedience,

- prompt language,

- front-end filtering,

- or developer convention.

Sensitive controls require architecture such as:

- server-side authorisation,

- scoped credentials,

- policy enforcement,

- project-bound queries,

- secret isolation,

- signed callbacks,

- and audit evidence.

The Fabric shall assume that inputs may be malicious or misleading.

External content, tools and providers shall be treated as untrusted until validated.

<a id="section-3-70"></a>
## 3.70 Principle — External Content Is Untrusted Input

Documents, web pages, emails, media and provider outputs may contain instructions intended to manipulate agents or tools.

The Fabric shall distinguish:

- user instruction,

- system policy,

- trusted project context,

- retrieved evidence,

- and untrusted external content.

Retrieved content shall not automatically gain execution authority.

Prompt injection resistance is part of capability execution, especially for:

- research,

- browsing,

- document analysis,

- and tool use.

<a id="section-3-71"></a>
## 3.71 Principle — Tool Use Requires Separate Authority

Permission to reason does not imply permission to act.

A model may analyse a task without authority to invoke tools.

Tool permissions shall be explicit by:

- agent,

- capability,

- project,

- action,

- environment,

- and risk.

Read, create, modify, delete, publish and administer are separate action classes.

A provider connection shall not automatically authorise all exposed tools.

<a id="section-3-72"></a>
## 3.72 Principle — Side Effects Are First-Class

Actions that change external state shall be represented explicitly.

Examples include:

- sending a message,

- publishing content,

- creating a provider asset,

- charging credits,

- deleting a file,

- changing configuration,

- committing code,

- and scheduling media.

Side effects require:

- identity,

- authority,

- idempotency strategy,

- evidence,

- and completion verification.

They shall not be hidden inside a generic generation request.

<a id="section-3-73"></a>
## 3.73 Principle — Canonical Assets Are Protected

The Fabric may create drafts and derivatives.

It shall not overwrite canonical project assets without explicit authority.

Canonical assets may include:

- Nova and Pling character references,

- approved logos,

- master narration,

- canonical manuscripts,

- production templates,

- and final published masters.

New output should normally create a new version or derivative relationship.

Destructive replacement requires stronger approval.

<a id="section-3-74"></a>
## 3.74 Principle — Publication Is a Separate Consequence

Generation and publication shall remain separate capabilities.

An agent may be authorised to create marketing material without being authorised to publish it.

A production envelope may include publication authority, but that authority must be explicit.

The system shall preserve separate states for:

- draft,

- reviewed,

- approved,

- ready,

- scheduled,

- published,

- and verified.

Public consequence must never be inferred from creative completion.

<a id="section-3-75"></a>
## 3.75 Principle — User Override Is Governed

Users may override routing recommendations.

A user may choose:

- a specific provider,

- a premium model,

- local execution,

- lower cost,

- or manual handling.

The override must still respect:

- law,

- tenant policy,

- security,

- rights,

- and technical compatibility.

Overrides should be recorded when they materially affect cost, quality or risk.

User authority is primary.

It is not equivalent to bypassing all platform safeguards.

<a id="section-3-76"></a>
## 3.76 Principle — Recommendations Must Explain Trade-Offs

When the Fabric or Atlas recommends an execution path, the recommendation should explain relevant trade-offs.

Examples include:

- lower cost but slower execution,

- higher quality but greater credit use,

- private execution but reduced capability,

- premium video for key scenes and cheaper models for derivatives,

- or provider pinning with weaker fallback.

The purpose of explanation is to support informed approval.

The system should not overwhelm users with provider detail when it does not affect the decision.

<a id="section-3-77"></a>
## 3.77 Principle — Optimisation Must Serve the Approved Objective

The Fabric may optimise:

- cost,

- latency,

- quality,

- reliability,

- privacy,

- or resource utilisation.

Optimisation shall remain subordinate to the approved objective.

The system must not reduce visual quality merely to improve cost metrics when brand quality is essential.

It must not use expensive premium resources merely to maximise a quality score when the user selected Economy.

Metrics are instruments.

They are not the purpose of the system.

<a id="section-3-78"></a>
## 3.78 Principle — No Commission-Driven Routing

If Omnira later receives commercial benefit from particular providers, routing shall remain aligned with the user’s approved objective.

Provider commission, partnership or margin must not silently override:

- quality,

- cost,

- privacy,

- reliability,

- or user preference.

Commercial relationships shall be governed transparently.

The Fabric must not become a marketplace that prioritises platform revenue over user value.

<a id="section-3-79"></a>
## 3.79 Principle — Free Tiers Are Temporary Conditions

Free capacity may be useful.

It shall not be treated as a permanent architectural guarantee.

Free tiers may change in:

- availability,

- limits,

- rights,

- API access,

- quality,

- and provider policy.

The Fabric shall model free capacity as current provider-account state.

Workflows must remain capable of handling its exhaustion or removal.

The platform’s mission is low-barrier access.

It is not dependence on unstable promotional capacity.

<a id="section-3-80"></a>
## 3.80 Principle — Stage-Based Delivery

The target architecture may be broad.

Implementation shall proceed through bounded stages.

Each stage must define:

- included capabilities,

- excluded capabilities,

- security controls,

- migration impact,

- tests,

- acceptance criteria,

- and operational limits.

Future architecture shall not be represented as current capability.

Stage 1 must establish trustworthy contracts before broad automation.

Expansion follows evidence.

<a id="section-3-81"></a>
## 3.81 Principle — A Small Governed Core Is Better Than Broad Ungoverned Coverage

The first implementation should integrate a limited number of representative resources correctly.

It is better to have:

- two governed text providers,

- one local runtime,

- one voice provider,

- one image provider,

- and one multimodal provider,

than dozens of integrations without consistent:

- scope,

- cost,

- policy,

- evidence,

- and failure handling.

Provider count is not the success metric.

Architectural conformance is.

<a id="section-3-82"></a>
## 3.82 Principle — Conformance Must Be Testable

Architectural principles shall be translated into verifiable controls.

Conformance tests may validate:

- missing project scope fails closed,

- ineligible providers are rejected,

- confidential data cannot route publicly,

- generation approval does not permit publication,

- retries do not duplicate cost unexpectedly,

- provider accounts remain isolated,

- manual integrations are labelled correctly,

- and agent identity survives model changes.

A principle that cannot be verified should be refined until its operational meaning is clear.

<a id="section-3-83"></a>
## 3.83 Principle — Documentation Does Not Equal Implementation

Architecture documents define intended behaviour.

They do not prove runtime behaviour.

A capability may be:

- documented,

- specified,

- coded,

- tested,

- deployed,

- operational,

- or governed.

These states shall remain separate.

The Fabric interface and Atlas must not claim that a documented provider or capability is available until runtime evidence supports the claim.

<a id="section-3-84"></a>
## 3.84 Principle — The Runtime Is Authoritative for Current Capability

The canonical architecture governs intended direction.

The verified runtime governs what currently exists.

When the book and runtime differ:

- the book may identify a missing implementation,

- the runtime may reveal a defect,

- or the architecture may require revision.

The system must not hide the difference.

Architecture truth and operational truth are related but distinct.

<a id="section-3-85"></a>
## 3.85 Principle — Changes Require Provenance

Material changes to the Fabric architecture or runtime shall preserve:

- initiator,

- reason,

- affected scope,

- previous state,

- new state,

- approval,

- tests,

- migration,

- and rollback.

This applies to:

- providers,

- models,

- adapters,

- policies,

- budgets,

- data placement,

- and routing.

Change without provenance creates hidden operational risk.

<a id="section-3-86"></a>
## 3.86 Principle — Incident Response Preserves Evidence

During incidents, the Fabric shall prioritise:

- containment,

- truthful status,

- evidence preservation,

- scope identification,

- and safe recovery.

Incident response must not destroy the information needed to understand what happened.

Emergency action may temporarily disable providers, models, workflows or credentials.

Emergency authority must be explicit and reviewed afterward.

<a id="section-3-87"></a>
## 3.87 Principle — Provider Failure Must Not Become Project Corruption

A provider failure should not corrupt:

- project state,

- canonical assets,

- budgets,

- memory,

- or unrelated executions.

Execution boundaries should isolate failure.

Partially created assets shall remain identifiable as incomplete.

Unconfirmed costs shall remain unresolved rather than guessed.

Provider-specific defects shall not spread silently through the platform.

<a id="section-3-88"></a>
## 3.88 Principle — User Experience Must Preserve Critical Truth

The Fabric should simplify complexity for users.

It must still surface decisions that materially affect:

- money,

- privacy,

- rights,

- quality,

- publication,

- and authority.

The interface need not expose every provider parameter.

It must expose enough information for meaningful approval.

Simplicity must not become concealment.

<a id="section-3-89"></a>
## 3.89 Principle — Machine Consumers Do Not Gain Authority From Documentation

Atlas, Claude, Codex and future agents may consume this book.

Access to architectural knowledge does not grant:

- credentials,

- execution rights,

- budget,

- publication authority,

- or administrative permission.

Machine consumers shall treat the book as normative context subject to runtime controls.

Knowledge and authority remain separate.

<a id="section-3-90"></a>
## 3.90 Anti-Principle — Provider-Centric Architecture

The Fabric must not be designed around the permanent assumptions of one provider.

Provider-centric architecture typically appears as:

- provider model names inside business logic,

- provider-specific errors throughout applications,

- direct credentials in project code,

- provider conversations used as canonical memory,

- and no meaningful exit path.

This anti-pattern is prohibited.

<a id="section-3-91"></a>
## 3.91 Anti-Principle — Prompt-Only Governance

Important controls must not exist only as natural-language instructions.

Prompt policies may guide agents.

They cannot guarantee:

- project isolation,

- budget limits,

- secret protection,

- publication authority,

- or provider eligibility.

Governance must be enforced by architecture where consequences require it.

<a id="section-3-92"></a>
## 3.92 Anti-Principle — Cheapest at Any Cost

The Fabric must not optimise only for lowest provider price.

This produces false economy when the result is:

- unusable,

- unsafe,

- legally restricted,

- repeatedly regenerated,

- or expensive to correct.

Economy routing must still satisfy the quality contract.

<a id="section-3-93"></a>
## 3.93 Anti-Principle — Premium by Default

The Fabric must not assume that the most expensive model is always the correct choice.

Premium resources should be used when justified by:

- quality,

- risk,

- complexity,

- or user preference.

Routine work may be better served by local or lower-cost resources.

The system shall optimise intentionally rather than perform prestige routing.

<a id="section-3-94"></a>
## 3.94 Anti-Principle — Silent Provider Fallback

Fallback must not occur invisibly when it changes:

- cost,

- quality,

- privacy,

- rights,

- or user expectation.

Material fallback requires disclosure or approval according to policy.

A hidden provider substitution weakens trust and provenance.

<a id="section-3-95"></a>
## 3.95 Anti-Principle — Unbounded Context Sharing

The Fabric must not provide every agent and provider with all available project context.

Context should be selected according to need.

Broad context sharing increases risk and often reduces reasoning quality.

Retrieval and memory access shall remain scoped.

<a id="section-3-96"></a>
## 3.96 Anti-Principle — Automatic Memory Dumping

The Fabric must not save every prompt, result and error as permanent memory.

Such behaviour creates a noisy and unsafe memory system.

Evidence shall be retained according to its operational value.

Memory shall be admitted through governed learning.

<a id="section-3-97"></a>
## 3.97 Anti-Principle — False Automation

A workflow must not be described as autonomous when a human performs essential undisclosed steps.

Human-assisted operation is valid.

False automation is not.

Integration maturity shall be represented truthfully.

<a id="section-3-98"></a>
## 3.98 Anti-Principle — Successful Call Equals Successful Outcome

A successful API response may indicate only that the provider accepted a request.

The Fabric must not confuse:

- transport success,

- provider acceptance,

- output receipt,

- validation,

- approval,

- and final business outcome.

Completion contracts shall define the required proof.

<a id="section-3-99"></a>
## 3.99 Anti-Principle — Global Scope as Convenience

The Fabric must not use global scope because project resolution is difficult.

Global scope is a privileged operational state.

It requires explicit purpose and authority.

Missing project scope shall fail closed.

<a id="section-3-100"></a>
## 3.100 Anti-Principle — One Agent, One Model

An agent must not be permanently defined by one provider model.

This creates brittle identity, weak fallback and vendor lock-in.

Agent continuity belongs to Omnira.

<a id="section-3-101"></a>
## 3.101 Anti-Principle — One Model for Every Task

No single model shall be assumed to be optimal for all capabilities.

The Fabric exists partly because intelligence work is heterogeneous.

Different tasks may require different:

- models,

- providers,

- tools,

- locations,

- and evaluation methods.

<a id="section-3-102"></a>
## 3.102 Anti-Principle — Hidden Irreversible Actions

Deletion, publication, spending, credential changes and other irreversible actions shall not be hidden inside ordinary workflows.

They must be identifiable, authorised and evidenced.

Convenience does not justify concealed consequence.

<a id="section-3-103"></a>
## 3.103 Resolving Doctrine Conflicts

When two principles appear to conflict, the system shall identify the conflict explicitly.

Examples include:

- lower cost versus higher quality,

- private execution versus faster external execution,

- provider pinning versus outage recovery,

- evidence retention versus privacy minimisation,

- automation versus human approval.

Resolution should use:

1. mandatory law and rights,

2. explicit user and tenant policy,

3. data classification,

4. approved objective,

5. authority envelope,

6. routing profile,

7. documented trade-offs,

8. and escalation where necessary.

The Fabric shall not silently choose the most convenient interpretation.

<a id="section-3-104"></a>
## 3.104 Doctrine Applied to Stage 1

Stage 1 shall implement the doctrine through a limited but complete execution path.

At minimum, Stage 1 must demonstrate:

- capability-first requests,

- explicit tenant and project scope,

- provider and account separation,

- model-independent agent identity,

- policy evaluation,

- data classification,

- cost estimation,

- bounded approval,

- truthful state,

- execution evidence,

- provenance,

- bounded retries,

- and policy-compliant fallback.

Stage 1 may leave advanced capabilities manual.

It must identify them honestly.

Stage 1 shall not activate unrestricted autonomous production.

<a id="section-3-105"></a>
## 3.105 Stage 1 Doctrine Acceptance Tests

Representative acceptance tests should include:

1. A request without tenant scope is rejected.

2. A project-scoped request cannot access another project’s assets.

3. A Local Only request cannot route to an external provider.

4. An expired approval blocks paid generation.

5. A generation-only approval cannot trigger publication.

6. A failed provider may fall back only to an eligible provider.

7. A retry does not duplicate a confirmed completed execution.

8. An uncertain provider result becomes unknown outcome.

9. Provider cost is attributed to the correct project and account.

10. A provider model may be changed without changing the agent identity.

11. Raw credentials do not enter prompts or logs.

12. A manual integration is labelled as human-assisted.

13. An output retains provenance after storage.

14. A raw execution log does not automatically become permanent memory.

15. Policy and adapter versions are recorded in the execution evidence.

Passing these tests does not prove complete conformance.

Failure indicates a material doctrine violation.

9. Least privilege SHALL apply to callers, agents, tools and provider accounts.

13. Quality SHALL be defined through task-specific contracts.

14. Routing SHALL select the cheapest eligible path, not merely the cheapest available resource.

18. Approval SHALL be action-specific and scope-specific.

19. Delegation SHALL NOT create new authority.

20. Providers SHALL NOT receive independent business authority.

21. Meaningful execution SHALL produce appropriate evidence.

22. Meaningful outputs SHALL retain provenance.

23. Logs SHALL NOT automatically become memory.

25. Retries SHALL be bounded and idempotency-aware.

28. Local and private resources SHALL be first-class.

30. Provider-specific logic SHALL remain isolated behind adapters or explicit extensions.

31. Protocol choice SHALL NOT determine trust.

<a id="section-3-107"></a>
## 3.107 Doctrine Invariants

The following invariants shall remain true throughout future evolution:

- No provider becomes the owner of Omnira’s intelligence architecture.

- No model becomes the permanent identity of an Omnira agent.

- No project gains access to another project merely because infrastructure is shared.

- No paid execution occurs without an identifiable budget source.

- No protected data leaves its permitted processing boundary.

- No approval is silently broadened.

- No fallback weakens mandatory policy.

- No generated output becomes canonical merely because it exists.

- No execution log becomes memory merely because it was recorded.

- No manual action is represented as autonomous execution.

- No uncertain external result is represented as verified success.

- No future capability is represented as currently implemented without evidence.

- No component expands its own authority.

- No optimisation objective overrides legitimate human ownership.

## 3.108 Closing Statement

The doctrine of the Omnira Intelligence Fabric exists to ensure that increased intelligence capability produces increased human leverage rather than increased dependency, opacity or risk.

The Fabric shall make advanced technology easier to use without making its consequences invisible.

It shall reduce cost without sacrificing required quality.

It shall enable autonomy without surrendering authority.

It shall support learning without allowing uncontrolled memory drift.

It shall integrate providers without allowing them to own the platform.

It shall share infrastructure without mixing projects.

It shall automate execution without falsifying completion.

It shall evolve rapidly while preserving stable architectural truth.

No Intelligence Without Fabric.

No Fabric execution without scope.

No consequential action without authority.

No accepted output without evidence.

No learning without provenance.

No optimisation without purpose.

The user’s vision remains primary.

Omnira remains the owner of its intelligence architecture.

The Fabric remains the governed path between them.
