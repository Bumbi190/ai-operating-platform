# Chapter 13 — Quality, Evaluation and Benchmarking

<a id="section-13-1"></a>
## 13.1 Chapter Status

This chapter defines the canonical architecture, governance model, lifecycle, contracts and operational responsibilities for quality, evaluation, benchmarking and acceptance within the Omnira Intelligence Fabric.

It establishes:

- how quality is defined for intelligence work,

- how Quality Floors, Quality Targets and Acceptance Criteria remain distinct,

- how quality requirements are attached to Capability Requests, Plans, Steps, Outputs and Assets,

- how deterministic, model-based, human and outcome-based evaluation methods coexist,

- how evaluation remains independent from generation where consequence requires it,

- how benchmarks are designed, versioned, executed, interpreted and retired,

- how Model, Provider, Tool, Agent and Workflow performance is measured,

- how Project-specific and platform-wide evidence remain correctly scoped,

- how factuality, grounding, reasoning, code, image, video, voice, audio and multimodal quality are evaluated,

- how child-facing content receives stronger review,

- how evaluator bias, uncertainty and disagreement are represented,

- how quality evidence affects routing without becoming universal truth,

- how rejected Outputs, retries and fallback remain attributable,

- how accepted Outputs become Asset Candidates without bypassing rights or authority,

- how production monitoring detects quality drift,

- how evaluation learning is governed,

- and how Stage 1 shall implement the smallest trustworthy quality system.

Quality is not one universal score.

Quality is the degree to which a bounded Output satisfies the requirements of a specific Capability, request, Project and intended use.

A Model may be excellent for one task and unsuitable for another.

A Provider may be operationally reliable while producing weak Project-specific results.

An Output may be technically valid but factually wrong.

An image may be visually attractive but inconsistent with canonical characters.

A narration may be intelligible but unsuitable for Swedish child-facing content.

A code patch may compile while violating architecture.

A generated Asset may pass quality review while lacking commercial rights.

Evaluation therefore determines whether the Output is acceptable for its intended next state.

It does not replace:

- policy,

- authority,

- rights,

- consent,

- security,

- provenance,

- or publication approval.

This chapter is normative after formal approval.

Implementation and conformance must be verified separately.

<a id="section-13-2"></a>
## 13.2 Purpose of the Quality System

The Intelligence Fabric may generate or transform:

- text,

- code,

- images,

- audio,

- voice,

- music,

- video,

- structured data,

- plans,

- decisions,

- summaries,

- classifications,

- recommendations,

- and external side effects.

Provider success confirms only that the Provider accepted or completed an operation.

It does not prove that the result:

- satisfies the user’s intent,

- meets the Capability Contract,

- is factually supported,

- follows Project identity,

- preserves canonical meaning,

- is safe for the intended audience,

- is commercially usable,

- or is ready for publication.

The quality system exists to create explicit evidence for those decisions.

<a id="section-13-3"></a>
## 13.3 Quality Is Contextual

Quality shall always be interpreted relative to:

- Capability,

- Input,

- Output Contract,

- Project,

- audience,

- consequence,

- Quality Contract,

- environment,

- and intended next action.

A concise caption and a canonical architecture chapter require different evaluation.

A background animation and a close-up of Nova and Pling require different visual criteria.

A private draft and a public news report require different factual standards.

A prototype patch and a production merge require different software validation.

<a id="section-13-4"></a>
## 13.4 Quality Is Not Provider Reputation

Provider reputation may support initial confidence.

It does not prove suitability for the current task.

The quality system shall prefer:

- direct evidence,

- Capability-specific benchmarks,

- Project-specific evaluations,

- recent production outcomes,

- and attributable human review.

<a id="section-13-5"></a>
## 13.5 Quality Is Not Price

Higher price does not prove higher quality.

Lower price does not prove inadequate quality.

Price may correlate with:

- capacity,

- Features,

- latency,

- or Provider positioning.

Quality must still be measured.

<a id="section-13-6"></a>
## 13.6 Quality Is Not User Preference Alone

User preference is an important signal.

It may capture:

- style,

- tone,

- familiarity,

- emotional fit,

- and brand alignment.

Preference alone may not prove:

- factual correctness,

- technical validity,

- safety,

- rights,

- or conformance.

<a id="section-13-7"></a>
## 13.7 Quality Is Not Acceptance Alone

An Output may be accepted for practical reasons while known limitations remain.

Acceptance shall preserve:

- criteria,

- exceptions,

- reviewer,

- context,

- and intended use.

One accepted Output shall not automatically prove general Model superiority.

<a id="section-13-8"></a>
## 13.8 Quality Is Not Correctness Alone

Correctness is one quality dimension.

Other dimensions may include:

- relevance,

- completeness,

- clarity,

- style,

- consistency,

- usefulness,

- safety,

- latency,

- reproducibility,

- and user effort.

<a id="section-13-9"></a>
## 13.9 Quality Is Not Safety

Safety and quality overlap but remain distinct.

An Output may be:

- safe but low quality,

- high quality but unsafe,

- or both acceptable.

Mandatory safety policy shall not be reduced to a quality score.

<a id="section-13-10"></a>
## 13.10 Quality Is Not Rights

An excellent Output may still be ineligible for commercial use.

Rights and consent remain separate mandatory controls.

<a id="section-13-11"></a>
## 13.11 Quality Is Not Publication Approval

An Output may pass quality evaluation and still require:

- owner approval,

- legal review,

- brand approval,

- or publication authority.

Quality acceptance prepares the Output for its next permitted state.

<a id="section-13-14"></a>
## 13.14 Quality Dimension

A Quality Dimension is one bounded property evaluated for an Output or resource.

Examples include:

- factual accuracy,

- source support,

- relevance,

- completeness,

- instruction adherence,

- schema validity,

- code correctness,

- character consistency,

- pronunciation,

- motion coherence,

- child appropriateness,

- latency,

- or acceptance probability.

<a id="section-13-15"></a>
## 13.15 Quality Dimension Identity

Every reusable Quality Dimension shall have stable canonical identity.

The identity shall remain independent of:

- evaluator,

- metric,

- Project,

- Model,

- Provider,

- and current threshold.

<a id="section-13-16"></a>
## 13.16 Dimension Definition

A Quality Dimension definition should include:

- dimensionId,

- canonical name,

- description,

- applicable Capabilities,

- measurement types,

- direction,

- known limitations,

- and provenance.

<a id="section-13-17"></a>
## 13.17 Dimension Direction

A Dimension shall define whether:

- higher is better,

- lower is better,

- exact match is required,

- pass or fail applies,

- or categorical interpretation is used.

<a id="section-13-18"></a>
## 13.18 Dimension Applicability

Not every Dimension applies to every Capability.

For example:

- pronunciation applies to speech,

- character identity applies to visual media,

- compilation applies to code,

- citation support applies to grounded research.

<a id="section-13-19"></a>
## 13.19 Quality Contract

A Quality Contract is the canonical set of quality requirements for a request, Plan, Step, Output or Asset.

It shall include or reference:

- qualityContractId,

- scope,

- Capability,

- Dimensions,

- Quality Floors,

- Quality Targets,

- Acceptance Criteria,

- evaluator requirements,

- review requirements,

- confidence requirements,

- allowed exceptions,

- and lifecycle.

<a id="section-13-20"></a>
## 13.20 Quality Contract Identity

Every persistent Quality Contract shall have stable identity and version.

Executions and Evaluations shall reference the exact version used.

<a id="section-13-21"></a>
## 13.21 Quality Contract Scope

A Quality Contract may apply to:

- Platform,

- Tenant,

- Project,

- Workflow,

- Production,

- Plan,

- Step,

- Capability,

- Output type,

- Asset type,

- or audience.

<a id="section-13-22"></a>
## 13.22 Platform Quality Contract

A Platform Quality Contract defines universal expectations such as:

- no false completion,

- schema conformance where required,

- evidence preservation,

- and truthful uncertainty.

<a id="section-13-23"></a>
## 13.23 Tenant Quality Contract

A Tenant may define organisation-level standards.

Project requirements may be stricter.

<a id="section-13-24"></a>
## 13.24 Project Quality Contract

A Project Quality Contract defines Project-specific quality requirements.

Examples include:

- Nova and Pling visual identity,

- The Prompt factual sourcing,

- GainPilot training-language accuracy,

- Omnira architecture conformance.

<a id="section-13-25"></a>
## 13.25 Workflow Quality Contract

A Workflow may define quality requirements for every Run.

The Workflow Contract shall not replace request-specific additions.

<a id="section-13-26"></a>
## 13.26 Production Quality Contract

A Production Quality Contract applies to a complete deliverable.

It may aggregate:

- master Asset quality,

- derivative quality,

- narration quality,

- technical conformance,

- and human approval.

<a id="section-13-27"></a>
## 13.27 Step Quality Contract

A Step Quality Contract evaluates one bounded Output.

Step-level evaluation supports:

- retry,

- fallback,

- and selective correction.

<a id="section-13-28"></a>
## 13.28 Asset Quality Contract

An Asset Quality Contract defines the criteria required before an Asset may become:

- accepted,

- canonical,

- publishable,

- or reusable.

<a id="section-13-29"></a>
## 13.29 Quality Contract Composition

The effective Quality Contract may compose requirements from:

1. Platform.

2. Tenant.

3. Project.

4. Capability.

5. Workflow.

6. Production.

7. Plan.

8. Step.

9. Request-specific additions.

A lower scope may strengthen requirements.

It shall not weaken mandatory higher-level Floors without authority.

<a id="section-13-30"></a>
## 13.30 Quality Floor

A Quality Floor is the minimum acceptable level for one Dimension or composite gate.

A candidate or Output below the Floor is not acceptable for the defined use.

<a id="section-13-31"></a>
## 13.31 Quality Target

A Quality Target is the desired level above the minimum Floor.

Routing and Premium optimisation may use the Target.

<a id="section-13-32"></a>
## 13.32 Quality Ceiling

A Quality Ceiling may define a point beyond which additional quality effort is not economically justified.

It may help prevent:

- excessive retries,

- unnecessary premium escalation,

- and perfection loops.

<a id="section-13-33"></a>
## 13.33 Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance Criteria define the exact conditions under which an Output may advance.

They may include:

- all mandatory checks pass,

- composite score exceeds threshold,

- human reviewer approves,

- no critical defects remain,

- required rights are valid,

- and required evidence exists.

<a id="section-13-34"></a>
## 13.34 Acceptance Criteria Versus Quality Floor

A Quality Floor applies to a Dimension.

Acceptance Criteria define the complete decision logic.

For example:

- every technical check must pass,

- character consistency score must exceed threshold,

- and a human reviewer must approve.

<a id="section-13-35"></a>
## 13.35 Hard Quality Requirement

A Hard Quality Requirement cannot be traded away.

Examples include:

- valid schema,

- correct file format,

- no missing mandatory section,

- exact canonical character identity,

- or successful type check where required.

<a id="section-13-36"></a>
## 13.36 Soft Quality Preference

A Soft Quality Preference influences selection or review without causing automatic rejection.

Examples include:

- slightly warmer tone,

- more cinematic motion,

- shorter response,

- or more energetic pacing.

<a id="section-13-37"></a>
## 13.37 Critical Quality Dimension

A Critical Dimension is one whose failure blocks acceptance.

Examples may include:

- factual support for public news,

- safety for child-facing media,

- compilation for production code,

- consent for a cloned voice.

<a id="section-13-38"></a>
## 13.38 Advisory Quality Dimension

An Advisory Dimension provides useful information but does not alone determine acceptance.

<a id="section-13-39"></a>
## 13.39 Composite Quality Requirement

Several Dimensions may form a composite requirement.

The composition rule shall be explicit.

<a id="section-13-40"></a>
## 13.40 Quality Threshold

A Threshold maps a Score or categorical result to a decision boundary.

It shall include:

- thresholdId,

- Dimension,

- scope,

- value,

- comparison,

- confidence requirement,

- version,

- and provenance.

<a id="section-13-41"></a>
## 13.41 Static Threshold

A Static Threshold remains fixed until governance changes it.

<a id="section-13-42"></a>
## 13.42 Calibrated Threshold

A Calibrated Threshold may be adjusted based on validated evaluation evidence.

Changes require versioning.

<a id="section-13-43"></a>
## 13.43 Project-Specific Threshold

A Project may require stricter or differently weighted thresholds.

<a id="section-13-44"></a>
## 13.44 Audience-Specific Threshold

Public, child-facing, medical, financial or high-impact use may require stronger thresholds.

<a id="section-13-45"></a>
## 13.45 Environment-Specific Threshold

Development may permit lower acceptance for experiments.

Production shall use approved production thresholds.

<a id="section-13-46"></a>
## 13.46 Evaluation

Evaluation is the governed process of measuring an Output, resource or system against defined criteria.

Evaluation may be:

- deterministic,

- statistical,

- model-based,

- human,

- comparative,

- adversarial,

- operational,

- or outcome-based.

<a id="section-13-47"></a>
## 13.47 Evaluation Target

An Evaluation Target may be:

- Output,

- Asset,

- Model Version,

- Model Deployment,

- Provider,

- Tool,

- Adapter,

- Agent,

- Workflow,

- Plan,

- Routing Profile,

- or complete Production.

<a id="section-13-48"></a>
## 13.48 Evaluation Plan

An Evaluation Plan defines how a target shall be evaluated.

It shall include or reference:

- evaluationPlanId,

- target type,

- Quality Contract,

- Evaluation Methods,

- Evaluators,

- Test Cases,

- sampling,

- thresholds,

- review rules,

- disagreement handling,

- cost,

- deadline,

- and provenance.

<a id="section-13-49"></a>
## 13.49 Evaluation Plan Identity

Every persistent Evaluation Plan shall have stable identity and version.

<a id="section-13-50"></a>
## 13.50 Evaluation Plan Scope

Evaluation Plans may be:

- production-specific,

- Capability-specific,

- Project-specific,

- benchmark-specific,

- or continuous-monitoring plans.

<a id="section-13-51"></a>
## 13.51 Evaluation Method

An Evaluation Method defines the procedure used to measure one or more Dimensions.

Methods may include:

- exact comparison,

- schema validation,

- static analysis,

- test execution,

- metric calculation,

- model grading,

- pairwise comparison,

- rubric review,

- human checklist,

- expert assessment,

- user feedback,

- and business-outcome analysis.

<a id="section-13-52"></a>
## 13.52 Deterministic Evaluation

Deterministic Evaluation produces the same result for the same Inputs and evaluator version under defined conditions.

Examples include:

- JSON schema validation,

- checksum verification,

- resolution check,

- duration check,

- compilation,

- type checking,

- and unit tests.

<a id="section-13-53"></a>
## 13.53 Statistical Evaluation

Statistical Evaluation measures performance across a sample.

Examples include:

- success rate,

- average score,

- confidence interval,

- error distribution,

- and percentile latency.

<a id="section-13-54"></a>
## 13.54 Model-Based Evaluation

Model-Based Evaluation uses one Model or model system to evaluate another Output or resource.

It may assess:

- relevance,

- style,

- factual support,

- visual consistency,

- or rubric conformance.

It shall preserve evaluator identity and limitations.

<a id="section-13-55"></a>
## 13.55 Human Evaluation

Human Evaluation uses an authorised person to assess quality.

It may be required for:

- creative judgement,

- brand identity,

- child-facing suitability,

- nuanced language,

- high-consequence claims,

- and publication readiness.

<a id="section-13-56"></a>
## 13.56 Comparative Evaluation

Comparative Evaluation ranks or selects among several Outputs.

It may use:

- pairwise preference,

- tournament,

- rubric scoring,

- or human selection.

<a id="section-13-57"></a>
## 13.57 Adversarial Evaluation

Adversarial Evaluation deliberately tests known weaknesses.

Examples include:

- prompt injection,

- malformed Input,

- ambiguous source,

- edge-case code,

- difficult character pose,

- or multilingual pronunciation.

<a id="section-13-58"></a>
## 13.58 Operational Evaluation

Operational Evaluation measures production behaviour such as:

- availability,

- retry rate,

- latency,

- cost variance,

- unknown-outcome rate,

- and acceptance rate.

<a id="section-13-59"></a>
## 13.59 Outcome-Based Evaluation

Outcome-Based Evaluation examines whether the Output achieved a later Project or business objective.

Examples include:

- user completion,

- audience engagement,

- reduced editing effort,

- or successful deployment.

Outcome evidence shall not be treated as direct proof of every quality Dimension.

<a id="section-13-60"></a>
## 13.60 Evaluation Independence

Evaluation should be independent from generation when consequence or bias warrants it.

Independence may mean:

- separate Model,

- separate Provider,

- deterministic test,

- human reviewer,

- or independent evidence source.

<a id="section-13-61"></a>
## 13.61 Generator Self-Evaluation

A generator may perform self-checking.

Self-evaluation may improve output.

It shall not be the sole acceptance evidence for high-consequence work.

<a id="section-13-62"></a>
## 13.62 Same-Model Evaluation

The same Model Version may evaluate its own Output only when:

- consequence is low,

- independent checks exist where required,

- and bias limitations are accepted.

<a id="section-13-63"></a>
## 13.63 Cross-Model Evaluation

A different Model may evaluate the Output.

Different Models may still share:

- Provider bias,

- training data,

- or similar failure modes.

<a id="section-13-64"></a>
## 13.64 Cross-Provider Evaluation

A separate Provider may increase independence.

It also changes:

- cost,

- privacy,

- and data custody.

Eligibility must be evaluated.

<a id="section-13-65"></a>
## 13.65 Deterministic and Model Composite

A common pattern is:

1. deterministic technical validation,

2. model-based semantic evaluation,

3. human approval where required.

Each layer provides different evidence.

<a id="section-13-66"></a>
## 13.66 Evaluation Cost

Evaluation consumes resources.

The Plan and Budget shall include:

- Model cost,

- Tool cost,

- human effort,

- and latency.

<a id="section-13-67"></a>
## 13.67 Evaluation Depth

Evaluation depth should reflect:

- consequence,

- cost,

- public exposure,

- irreversibility,

- and uncertainty.

<a id="section-13-68"></a>
## 13.68 Lightweight Evaluation

Low-risk drafts may use:

- schema validation,

- simple rubric,

- and user review.

<a id="section-13-69"></a>
## 13.69 Standard Evaluation

Ordinary production may use:

- deterministic checks,

- model evaluation,

- and sampled human review.

<a id="section-13-70"></a>
## 13.70 High-Assurance Evaluation

High-consequence work may require:

- multiple independent evaluators,

- primary-source verification,

- expert review,

- adversarial tests,

- and strict Completion Gates.

<a id="section-13-71"></a>
## 13.71 Evaluator

An Evaluator is a governed resource or Principal performing an Evaluation Method.

An Evaluator may be:

- deterministic Tool,

- Model Version,

- human reviewer,

- expert panel,

- external service,

- or outcome-analysis system.

<a id="section-13-72"></a>
## 13.72 Evaluator Identity

Every persistent Evaluator shall have stable identity.

The Evaluator shall remain distinct from:

- Evaluation Method,

- Test Suite,

- and Evaluation Result.

<a id="section-13-73"></a>
## 13.73 Model Evaluator

A Model Evaluator record should identify:

- Model Version,

- Provider,

- Deployment,

- prompt or rubric version,

- temperature or deterministic settings,

- and known limitations.

<a id="section-13-74"></a>
## 13.74 Tool Evaluator

A Tool Evaluator may perform:

- schema validation,

- code tests,

- malware scan,

- media inspection,

- or metric calculation.

Tool Version shall be preserved.

<a id="section-13-75"></a>
## 13.75 Human Reviewer

A Human Reviewer is a Principal authorised to evaluate specified quality Dimensions.

Reviewer metadata may include:

- reviewerId,

- role,

- expertise,

- Project scope,

- permitted data,

- and calibration status.

<a id="section-13-76"></a>
## 13.76 Expert Reviewer

An Expert Reviewer has domain expertise relevant to the evaluation.

Examples include:

- Swedish language reviewer,

- child-content reviewer,

- software architect,

- legal reviewer,

- or exercise professional.

<a id="section-13-77"></a>
## 13.77 Reviewer Assignment

Reviewer assignment shall consider:

- authority,

- expertise,

- Project scope,

- conflict of interest,

- availability,

- and data access.

<a id="section-13-78"></a>
## 13.78 Reviewer Independence

A creator may review their own work for low-risk purposes.

High-consequence acceptance may require another reviewer.

<a id="section-13-79"></a>
## 13.79 Reviewer Conflict of Interest

A conflict may exist when the reviewer:

- created the Output,

- owns the Provider relationship,

- benefits from acceptance,

- or lacks independence.

The conflict shall be disclosed where material.

<a id="section-13-80"></a>
## 13.80 Reviewer Calibration

Human and Model Evaluators may require calibration against:

- known examples,

- expert labels,

- or consensus sets.

Calibration evidence shall be versioned.

<a id="section-13-81"></a>
## 13.81 Evaluator Qualification

An Evaluator may be qualified for one Dimension but not another.

A visual Model may assess image artefacts but not commercial rights.

A code compiler can validate syntax but not business intent.

<a id="section-13-82"></a>
## 13.82 Evaluator Scope

Evaluator scope shall include:

- applicable Capabilities,

- Projects,

- Dimensions,

- consequence levels,

- and environments.

<a id="section-13-83"></a>
## 13.83 Evaluator Lifecycle

Evaluator lifecycle may include:

- proposed,

- calibrating,

- approved,

- active,

- restricted,

- degraded,

- suspended,

- deprecated,

- retired,

- and archived.

<a id="section-13-84"></a>
## 13.84 Evaluator Drift

Evaluator behaviour may change because of:

- Model update,

- rubric update,

- Tool update,

- reviewer fatigue,

- or Project-standard change.

Drift shall be monitored where material.

<a id="section-13-85"></a>
## 13.85 Evaluation Rubric

A Rubric defines how quality shall be judged.

It should include:

- Dimensions,

- definitions,

- scale,

- examples,

- unacceptable conditions,

- threshold interpretation,

- and reviewer instructions.

<a id="section-13-86"></a>
## 13.86 Rubric Identity

Every reusable Rubric shall have stable identity and version.

<a id="section-13-87"></a>
## 13.87 Rubric Scale

Scales may include:

- pass or fail,

- categorical,

- ordinal,

- numeric,

- pairwise preference,

- or ranked choice.

<a id="section-13-88"></a>
## 13.88 Numeric Scale

A numeric scale shall define:

- minimum,

- maximum,

- meaning,

- direction,

- and threshold.

<a id="section-13-89"></a>
## 13.89 Categorical Scale

Categories may include:

- unacceptable,

- weak,

- acceptable,

- strong,

- excellent,

- or unknown.

Category meaning shall be explicit.

<a id="section-13-90"></a>
## 13.90 Binary Evaluation

Pass or fail is appropriate for strict requirements.

Binary results may hide nuance and should be supplemented where useful.

<a id="section-13-91"></a>
## 13.91 Pairwise Preference

Pairwise evaluation compares Output A with Output B.

It shall define:

- criteria,

- tie handling,

- order randomisation,

- and evaluator identity.

<a id="section-13-92"></a>
## 13.92 Ranking Evaluation

Ranking orders several candidates.

Rankings shall remain scoped to the Test Cases and criteria used.

<a id="section-13-93"></a>
## 13.93 Rubric Examples

Examples may illustrate:

- passing result,

- borderline result,

- critical failure,

- and known ambiguity.

Examples support consistent evaluation.

<a id="section-13-94"></a>
## 13.94 Rubric Leakage

Benchmark rubrics and expected answers may leak into generation systems.

Leakage can invalidate results.

The architecture should separate production prompts from protected benchmark material where appropriate.

<a id="section-13-95"></a>
## 13.95 Evaluation Input

Evaluation Input may include:

- target Output,

- source Inputs,

- Capability Contract,

- Quality Contract,

- rubric,

- reference answer,

- canonical Asset references,

- provenance,

- and allowed context.

<a id="section-13-96"></a>
## 13.96 Evaluation Context Minimisation

Evaluators should receive only the context necessary to judge the target.

Excess context may:

- leak protected data,

- bias the evaluation,

- or increase cost.

- sample size,

- ambiguity,

- and disagreement.

- description,

- evidence,

<a id="section-13-115"></a>

- intermittent,

- input-specific,

- Provider,

- Adapter,

- prompt,

- Input,

- Tool,

- Plan,

- data,

- different Provider,

- rejected,

- internal use only,

- draft,

- preview,

- internal discussion,

- or test

but not for final publication.

The target is not yet acceptable but may become acceptable through correction.

<a id="section-13-126"></a>
## 13.126 Rejected

The target fails mandatory criteria or is unsuitable for the intended use.

<a id="section-13-127"></a>
## 13.127 Unresolved

Evidence is insufficient or Evaluators disagree materially.

<a id="section-13-128"></a>
## 13.128 Acceptance Validity

Acceptance may expire after:

- Output mutation,

- Model or source change,

- rights change,

- Project standard change,

- or defined period.

<a id="section-13-129"></a>
## 13.129 Acceptance Scope

Acceptance shall identify whether it applies to:

- one exact Output,

- one Asset Version,

- one derivative class,

- one Project,

- one audience,

- or one use.

<a id="section-13-130"></a>
## 13.130 Acceptance Does Not Propagate Automatically

Acceptance of one Output shall not automatically accept:

- edited versions,

- translated versions,

- derivatives,

- or later Provider regenerations.

<a id="section-13-135"></a>
## 13.135 Composite Gate

- revision,

## 13.191 Unknown-Outcome Rate

Unknown outcomes affect operational suitability.
