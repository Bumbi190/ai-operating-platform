# Chapter 25 — Observability, Audit and Incident Response

<a id="section-25-1"></a>
## 25.1 Chapter Status

This chapter defines the canonical architecture, object model, lifecycle, governance boundaries, operating practices and conformance requirements for observability, audit and Incident Response within the Omnira Intelligence Fabric.

It establishes:

- how the Fabric observes Requests, Plans, Executions, Attempts, Providers, Models, Tools, Agents, Nodes, queues, Assets, Memory and external side effects,

- how Metrics, logs, traces, Events, health signals, evidence and audit records remain distinct,

- how observability supports understanding without becoming an uncontrolled copy of protected data,

- how every material Execution receives end-to-end correlation,

- how technical state remains distinguishable from semantic success, accepted quality, external completion and business outcome,

- how service health, resource health, Provider health, Model health and workflow health are measured,

- how latency, throughput, saturation, error, quality, cost, privacy and authority signals remain visible,

- how telemetry remains scoped by Tenant, Project, Agent, Environment, resource and Classification,

- how structured logging avoids exposing full prompts, Outputs, private messages and Secrets,

- how distributed traces preserve causal execution paths across local and external systems,

- how Metrics and dashboards preserve operational truth without fabricating certainty,

- how alerting distinguishes symptoms, causes, risks, anomalies and confirmed Incidents,

- how alerts remain actionable, deduplicated, prioritised and owned,

- how audit records preserve who did what, to which object, under which authority, when and with what result,

- how audit differs from ordinary logs and immutable execution evidence,

- how user, Agent, administrator and emergency actions remain attributable,

- how audit integrity, retention, access and export are governed,

- how authority changes, approvals, data transfers, publication, deletion, Credential operations and policy changes receive elevated audit,

- how Incident detection, declaration, classification, containment, investigation, recovery and closure are governed,

- how privacy, security, reliability, quality, cost, rights, consent and authority Incidents remain distinguishable,

- how Incident severity and priority are determined,

- how command, ownership, communication and escalation operate,

- how evidence is preserved without exposing unnecessary content,

- how unknown outcomes, partial completion and delayed external effects are reconciled,

- how post-Incident learning becomes reviewed Derived Learning rather than folklore,

- how corrective actions remain owned, testable and traceable,

- how chaos testing, game days, fault injection and recovery exercises validate resilience,

- how Stage 1 shall implement the smallest trustworthy observability, audit and Incident Response foundation,

- and how future autonomy shall remain observable, stoppable and accountable.

A Metric is not an Event.

An Event is not a log line.

A log line is not an audit record.

An audit record is not complete execution evidence.

A trace is not proof of business completion.

A health check is not proof of semantic correctness.

An alert is not an Incident automatically.

An Incident is not merely an exception.

An error is not always a failure.

A failed Attempt is not always a failed Execution.

A successful Attempt is not always a successful Outcome.

A Provider-reported completion is not automatically an accepted Asset.

A dashboard is not the source of truth.

Absence of telemetry is not proof of health.

High availability is not correctness.

Low latency is not quality.

Averages are not sufficient for tail behaviour.

A high alert count is not proof of good monitoring.

A complete audit trail does not justify unrestricted access to its contents.

An administrator’s ability to operate infrastructure does not automatically permit reading user content.

Incident urgency does not erase classification, authority or evidence requirements.

This chapter is normative after formal approval.

Developer experience, testing and conformance are expanded in Chapter 26.

Stage 1 implementation architecture is expanded in Chapter 27.

Rollout, migration, anti-patterns and future evolution are expanded in Chapter 28.

Implementation and operational conformance must be verified separately.

<a id="section-25-2"></a>
## 25.2 Purpose of Observability, Audit and Incident Response

The Intelligence Fabric coordinates work across:

- user interfaces,

- Agents,

- Planner,

- Router,

- Scheduler,

- Admission Controller,

- Provider Adapters,

- Model Deployments,

- local Nodes,

- Tools,

- MCP servers,

- webhooks,

- queues,

- Asset stores,

- Memory,

- approval services,

- publication systems,

- and external platforms.

Those systems may fail through:

- unavailable Providers,

- incorrect Routing,

- queue backlog,

- latency spikes,

- Model regression,

- corrupted Assets,

- duplicate publication,

- wrong-Project access,

- Credential exposure,

- Budget overrun,

- stale approval,

- privacy leakage,

- rights failure,

- consent revocation,

- unknown external outcome,

- and human error.

Without canonical observability and Incident Response, Omnira could:

- know that something failed but not which Step,

- know that a Provider returned success but not whether publication occurred,

- detect high latency without seeing queue saturation,

- see cost growth without identifying retry storms,

- lose the relationship between a user action and an external side effect,

- hide privacy leakage inside ordinary logs,

- allow cross-Project retrieval to remain unnoticed,

- react repeatedly to duplicate alerts,

- restore service without resolving the underlying risk,

- or close an Incident without proving recovery.

The architecture exists to ensure that Omnira can answer:

- What is happening now?

- What happened previously?

- Which components participated?

- Which Tenant and Project were affected?

- Which Request, Plan, Execution and Attempt are involved?

- Which Provider, Account, Model, Tool and Node were used?

- Where is latency being introduced?

- Which queue or resource is saturated?

- Which error class occurred?

- Which authority and policy applied?

- Which data crossed which boundary?

- Which side effects occurred?

- Which outcomes remain unknown?

- Who detected the problem?

- Who owns the response?

- What was contained?

- What remains at risk?

- How was recovery verified?

- Which changes prevent recurrence?

- Which learning may safely influence future behaviour?

<a id="section-25-3"></a>
## 25.3 Observability

Observability is the capability to understand internal system state and behaviour from governed external signals and recorded evidence.

Observability shall support:

- detection,

- diagnosis,

- explanation,

- forecasting,

- capacity planning,

- reliability management,

- security monitoring,

- privacy monitoring,

- cost governance,

- and Incident Response.

<a id="section-25-4"></a>
## 25.4 Monitoring

Monitoring evaluates known signals, thresholds and expected conditions.

Monitoring is a subset of observability.

<a id="section-25-5"></a>
## 25.5 Telemetry

Telemetry is machine-produced operational data emitted by systems and resources.

Telemetry may include:

- Metrics,

- logs,

- traces,

- health observations,

- profiles,

- events,

- and resource measurements.

<a id="section-25-6"></a>
## 25.6 Metric

A Metric is a numerical measurement aggregated over dimensions and time.

Examples include:

- request count,

- latency,

- queue depth,

- error rate,

- token usage,

- cost,

- GPU utilisation,

- and approval delay.

<a id="section-25-7"></a>
## 25.7 Log

A log is a timestamped structured or textual record produced by a component.

Logs may support:

- debugging,

- operation,

- security,

- and reconstruction.

Logs are not automatically canonical evidence or audit records.

<a id="section-25-8"></a>
## 25.8 Trace

A Trace is a causal representation of one distributed operation across services, Steps, Attempts and external calls.

<a id="section-25-9"></a>
## 25.9 Span

A Span represents one bounded operation inside a Trace.

<a id="section-25-10"></a>
## 25.10 Event

An Event is a canonical statement that something meaningful occurred.

Examples include:

- Execution started,

- approval granted,

- Provider callback received,

- Asset accepted,

- publication verified,

- Grant revoked,

- or Incident declared.

<a id="section-25-11"></a>
## 25.11 Health Signal

A Health Signal describes the observed ability of a component or resource to serve its intended function.

<a id="section-25-12"></a>
## 25.12 Audit

Audit is the governed, attributable and reviewable record of material actions, Decisions, authority use and changes to protected or consequential state.

<a id="section-25-13"></a>
## 25.13 Incident

An Incident is an unplanned event or condition that causes or threatens material harm to:

- availability,

- reliability,

- correctness,

- privacy,

- security,

- rights,

- authority,

- cost,

- user trust,

- Project continuity,

- or external outcomes.

<a id="section-25-14"></a>
## 25.14 Incident Response

Incident Response is the governed lifecycle for:

- detection,

- triage,

- declaration,

- containment,

- investigation,

- recovery,

- communication,

- closure,

- and learning.

<a id="section-25-15"></a>

- Scheduling,

- Adapters,

- Credentials,

- Assets,

- Rights,

- Consent,

- Memory,

- Classification,

- Authority,

- Approvals,

- and Developer Conformance.

Every observability signal shall identify applicable scope where relevant.

- Tenant,

- Project,

- Environment,

- Agent,

- Request,

- Execution,

- Attempt,

- workflow,

- Provider,

- Account,

- Model,

- Tool,

- Node,

- queue,

- Asset,

- Memory Domain,

- and Incident.

<a id="section-25-18"></a>
## 25.18 Scope Before Aggregation

Aggregation shall not erase Tenant, Project or authority boundaries.

<a id="section-25-19"></a>
## 25.19 Scope Ambiguity

Ambiguous scope shall reduce trust and may require quarantine of the telemetry.

<a id="section-25-20"></a>
## 25.20 Correlation Context

Correlation Context is the canonical set of identifiers and Versions used to relate activity across components.

It should include or reference:

- correlationId,

- traceId,

- Request ID,

- Execution ID,

- Attempt ID,

- Tenant ID,

- Project ID,

- Agent ID,

- workflow ID,

- Plan Version,

- policy Versions,

- and Incident ID where applicable.

<a id="section-25-21"></a>
## 25.21 Correlation Context Identity

Every material operation shall carry stable correlation.

<a id="section-25-22"></a>
## 25.22 Correlation Propagation

Correlation shall propagate through:

- API calls,

- queue messages,

- webhook subscriptions,

- Provider requests,

- callbacks,

- local Tool invocations,

- Asset creation,

- audit records,

- and external verification.

<a id="section-25-23"></a>
## 25.23 Correlation Loss

Loss of correlation shall be visible as an observability defect.

<a id="section-25-24"></a>
## 25.24 External Correlation

Provider request IDs, job IDs and event IDs shall be linked to Omnira correlation without replacing canonical identity.

<a id="section-25-25"></a>
## 25.25 User Correlation

User-facing activity may reference a safe operation identifier without exposing internal infrastructure details.

<a id="section-25-26"></a>
## 25.26 Telemetry Record

A Telemetry Record is one governed operational observation.

It shall include or reference:

- telemetryRecordId,

- type,

- producer,

- scope,

- correlation,

- observedAt,

- recordedAt,

- value or payload,

- classification,

- integrity,

- retention,

- and provenance.

<a id="section-25-27"></a>
## 25.27 Telemetry Record Identity

Every persistent material Telemetry Record shall have stable identity or a stable series relationship.

<a id="section-25-28"></a>
## 25.28 Telemetry Producer

The producer may be:

- Service,

- Agent,

- Tool,

- Provider Adapter,

- Node,

- Runtime,

- database,

- queue,

- or external platform.

<a id="section-25-29"></a>
## 25.29 Observed Time

Observed time identifies when the state or event occurred.

<a id="section-25-30"></a>
## 25.30 Recorded Time

Recorded time identifies when Omnira stored the telemetry.

<a id="section-25-31"></a>
## 25.31 Ingestion Delay

The difference between observed and recorded time shall remain measurable.

<a id="section-25-32"></a>
## 25.32 Clock Source

Telemetry shall identify or inherit the relevant clock source.

<a id="section-25-33"></a>
## 25.33 Monotonic Duration

Elapsed time should be measured using a monotonic clock where possible.

<a id="section-25-34"></a>
## 25.34 Wall-Clock Timestamp

Human and distributed correlation requires an authoritative wall-clock timestamp.

<a id="section-25-35"></a>
## 25.35 Clock Skew

Clock skew shall be detected, bounded and visible.

<a id="section-25-36"></a>
## 25.36 Time Zone

Canonical operational timestamps should use UTC.

Local time may be displayed for users and schedules.

<a id="section-25-37"></a>
## 25.37 Time Precision

Precision shall match the operational need without creating misleading accuracy.

<a id="section-25-38"></a>
## 25.38 Telemetry Integrity

Material telemetry should include integrity controls appropriate to its risk.

<a id="section-25-39"></a>
## 25.39 Telemetry Loss

Telemetry loss shall be measured rather than hidden.

<a id="section-25-40"></a>
## 25.40 Telemetry Sampling

Sampling may reduce volume.

The sampling method and excluded signal classes shall remain explicit.

<a id="section-25-41"></a>
## 25.41 Mandatory Unsampled Signals

The following should not be omitted through ordinary sampling:

- authority changes,

- Approval Decisions,

- publication,

- deletion,

- Credential operations,

- privacy boundary violations,

- cross-Tenant access,

- Incident state changes,

- and emergency authority use.

<a id="section-25-42"></a>
## 25.42 Telemetry Classification

Telemetry inherits the sensitivity of included content and metadata.

<a id="section-25-43"></a>
## 25.43 Telemetry Minimisation

Operational usefulness shall be achieved with the minimum protected payload.

<a id="section-25-44"></a>
## 25.44 Metric Definition

A Metric Definition describes one canonical measurement.

It shall include or reference:

- metricDefinitionId,

- name,

- description,

- unit,

- type,

- dimensions,

- aggregation,

- source,

- scope,

- retention,

- and Version.

<a id="section-25-45"></a>
## 25.45 Metric Definition Identity

Every Metric Definition shall have stable identity and Version.

<a id="section-25-46"></a>
## 25.46 Metric Name

Metric names shall be:

- stable,

- meaningful,

- non-secret,

- and implementation-independent where practical.

<a id="section-25-47"></a>
## 25.47 Metric Unit

The unit shall be explicit.

Examples include:

- seconds,

- milliseconds,

- bytes,

- requests,

- tokens,

- currency,

- percentage,

- and count.

<a id="section-25-48"></a>
## 25.48 Metric Type

Metric types may include:

- counter,

- gauge,

- histogram,

- summary,

- distribution,

- rate,

- and derived ratio.

<a id="section-25-49"></a>
## 25.49 Counter

A Counter increases over time and may reset after process restart.

<a id="section-25-50"></a>
## 25.50 Gauge

A Gauge represents a current measured value.

Examples include:

- queue depth,

- active workers,

- memory usage,

- and remaining credits.

<a id="section-25-51"></a>
## 25.51 Histogram

A Histogram records a distribution over defined buckets.

<a id="section-25-52"></a>
## 25.52 Distribution

A Distribution preserves statistical shape across observations.

<a id="section-25-53"></a>
## 25.53 Rate

A Rate represents change per time unit.

<a id="section-25-54"></a>
## 25.54 Ratio

A Ratio compares two governed measurements.

The numerator and denominator shall be explicit.

<a id="section-25-55"></a>
## 25.55 Metric Point

A Metric Point records one value at one time and dimensional scope.

<a id="section-25-56"></a>
## 25.56 Metric Series

A Metric Series groups Metric Points sharing the same definition and dimensions.

<a id="section-25-57"></a>
## 25.57 Metric Dimension

Dimensions may include:

- Tenant,

- Project,

- Environment,

- Provider,

- Model,

- Capability,

- action class,

- error class,

- risk class,

- and result state.

<a id="section-25-58"></a>
## 25.58 High-Cardinality Dimension

High-cardinality values such as:

- user ID,

- Request ID,

- filename,

- URL,

- message ID,

- or Asset title

should not be ordinary Metric dimensions.

<a id="section-25-59"></a>
## 25.59 Metric Cardinality Control

Cardinality limits shall protect:

- cost,

- performance,

- privacy,

- and query reliability.

<a id="section-25-60"></a>
## 25.60 Metric Aggregation

Aggregation shall identify:

- function,

- interval,

- scope,

- and missing-data semantics.

<a id="section-25-61"></a>
## 25.61 Average

Average alone shall not be used to represent tail-sensitive performance.

<a id="section-25-62"></a>
## 25.62 Percentile

Percentiles such as:

- p50,

- p90,

- p95,

- p99,

- and p99.9

shall be used where latency or quality distributions matter.

<a id="section-25-63"></a>
## 25.63 Tail Latency

Tail latency shall be monitored separately from typical latency.

<a id="section-25-64"></a>
## 25.64 Missing Data

Missing Metric data shall remain distinct from zero.

<a id="section-25-65"></a>
## 25.65 Metric Freshness

Dashboards and Decisions shall display Metric freshness where material.

<a id="section-25-66"></a>
## 25.66 Derived Metric

A Derived Metric is calculated from canonical Metric Series.

Its formula and Version shall be explicit.

<a id="section-25-67"></a>
## 25.67 Metric Baseline

A baseline describes expected behaviour over a relevant period.

<a id="section-25-68"></a>
## 25.68 Baseline Scope

Baselines shall remain scoped to:

- resource,

- Environment,

- Project,

- Capability,

- and time pattern.

<a id="section-25-69"></a>
## 25.69 Seasonal Baseline

Time-of-day, weekday and campaign seasonality may affect expected values.

<a id="section-25-70"></a>
## 25.70 Static Threshold

A static threshold triggers when a Metric crosses a fixed boundary.

<a id="section-25-71"></a>
## 25.71 Dynamic Threshold

A dynamic threshold adapts to a governed baseline.

It shall preserve method, sensitivity and limitations.

<a id="section-25-72"></a>
## 25.72 Anomaly Score

An anomaly score indicates deviation.

It does not prove failure.

- errors,

- saturation,

- quality,

<a id="section-25-98"></a>

Authentication and Provider headers shall be redacted.

<a id="section-25-99"></a>

URLs may contain:

- Tokens,

- query data,

- filenames,

- user IDs,

- and signed access.

<a id="section-25-100"></a>
## 25.100 File Path Logging

File paths may reveal:

- user names,

- Project names,

- private directories,

- and internal architecture.

<a id="section-25-101"></a>
## 25.101 Error Logging

Errors shall preserve:

- class,

- code,

- component,

- operation,

- retryability,

- and correlation

without exposing unnecessary payload.

<a id="section-25-102"></a>
## 25.102 Stack Trace

Stack traces may be restricted to developer or Incident views.

<a id="section-25-103"></a>
## 25.103 Debug Logging

Debug logging shall be time-bounded and Environment-scoped.

<a id="section-25-104"></a>
## 25.104 Production Debug Capture

Expanded production capture requires:

- approval,

- Incident scope,

- short retention,

- access restriction,

- and cleanup.

<a id="section-25-105"></a>
## 25.105 Log Sampling

High-volume informational logs may be sampled.

<a id="section-25-106"></a>
## 25.106 Log Rotation

Log rotation shall prevent unbounded local storage growth.

<a id="section-25-107"></a>
## 25.107 Log Retention

Retention shall reflect:

- operational need,

- Classification,

- audit requirement,

- and privacy.

<a id="section-25-108"></a>

Material security and audit-supporting logs may require append-only or tamper-evident storage.

<a id="section-25-109"></a>
## 25.109 Log Access

Access shall be role-, Project-, Tenant- and purpose-scoped.

- widespread Tenant data exposure,

- loss of platform control,

- unrecoverable destruction of critical user data,

- or compromise of sovereign recovery authority.

<a id="section-25-329"></a>

- active cross-Tenant leakage,

- production Credential compromise,

- unauthorised public publication with major impact,

- or major service outage affecting critical operations.

- substantial Project outage,

- Confidential data sent to wrong Provider,

- repeated unauthorised spend,

- or accepted corrupt Assets.

- limited Project disruption,

- delayed publication,

- isolated quality failure,

- or recoverable workflow corruption.

<a id="section-25-332"></a>
## 25.332 SEV4 — Low

Examples may include:

- minor degraded experience,

- non-critical alerting defect,

- or limited internal workflow delay.

<a id="section-25-333"></a>
## 25.333 SEV5 — Informational Review

Examples may include:

- near miss,

- minor policy warning,

- or low-risk conformance observation.

Observability allows Omnira to understand what is happening.

Incident Response allows Omnira to contain harm, recover safely and improve without rewriting history.

The Metric measures behaviour.

The log records bounded operational detail.

The Trace preserves causal execution.

The Event records meaningful state change.

The Health Observation reports one measured condition.

The Health Evaluation determines whether a resource or service is fit for purpose.

The SLI defines what is measured.

The SLO defines the required objective.
